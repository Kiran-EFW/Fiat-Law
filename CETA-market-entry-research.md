# India–UK CETA: Market Research & Positioning Analysis for Fiat Law

**Prepared:** 4 August 2026
**Sources:** Primary throughout (gov.uk, PIB India, ONS, Companies House, SRA, legislation.gov.uk) plus Grant Thornton and CII published research. Items needing verification are flagged.

---

## 1. The headline: the window is open right now

The India–UK Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement (CETA) **entered into force on 15 July 2026** — three weeks ago. Signed 24 July 2025 after negotiations launched January 2022. The companion **Double Contribution Convention (DCC)** commenced the same day. Entry into force was briefly delayed by a dispute over UK steel safeguards introduced in March 2026, resolved in June.

The timing matters more than the contents. Every Indian business considering the UK is re-running its numbers this quarter, and advisory demand peaks in the 12–24 months after entry into force. Fiat Law is currently invisible in this market.

**Sources:** [gov.uk UK–India Trade Deal collection](https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/uk-india-trade-deal) · [UK Trade Tariff notice](https://www.trade-tariff.service.gov.uk/news/stories/india-free-trade-agreement-enters-into-force-on) · [PIB India, "India–UK CETA Comes into Effect", 15 July 2026](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2026/jul/doc2026715921801.pdf)

## 2. What the deal does

### Goods
- **99% of India's exports** to the UK get zero-duty access, covering close to 100% of trade value.
- India conceded on **89.5% of its tariff lines** (91% of UK exports), but only **24.5% of UK export value** gets immediate duty-free access; the rest phases over 5, 7 or 10 years.
- India protected dairy, cereals, pulses, apples, edible oils, gold, lab-grown diamonds, smartphones and optical fibres. Automotive uses a **37,000 passenger-vehicle CBU annual quota** rather than open liberalisation; EV concessions start only in Year 6.

### Services
- UK commitments across **all 12 major service sectors and 137 sub-sectors**, covering 99%+ of India's export interests. India reciprocated in 108.
- **Government procurement:** Indian suppliers gain access to the UK's **£90bn** procurement market.
- **Mutual Recognition Agreements** to be pursued within 12 months of entry into force — **by 15 July 2027** — for **nursing, accountancy and architecture**. Legal services are *not* on that list; the Bar Council of India's restrictions on foreign lawyers remain unresolved.

### Structural gap worth knowing
**There has been no India–UK bilateral investment treaty since India terminated it in March 2017.** A replacement is under negotiation. CETA did not fill this gap, so Indian investors in the UK have no investment-treaty protection — a point sophisticated clients will raise.

## 3. The Double Contribution Convention — the strongest commercial hook

**Before:** Indian professionals on short-term UK assignments and their employers paid roughly **23% of salary** into UK National Insurance, with no entitlement to UK benefits in return.

**Now:** dual contributions eliminated for assignments of up to **60 months**. The original text said 36 months; this was **extended to 60 months on 17 June 2026**, just before entry into force.

> Note: much published commentary still says "3 years." It is wrong. Being right about this in client-facing material is a cheap credibility win.

**Scale:** benefits **75,000+ Indian professionals and ~900 Indian companies**, with industry estimates of **USD 600m+ in annual savings**.

**Why it is the hook:** a 60-month NI exemption makes staffing a UK subsidiary with Indian employees dramatically cheaper than hiring locally. That decision drives sponsor licence work, ICT visa work and UK entity work — precisely the intersection of Janeevan John's actual expertise.

## 4. Mobility: what changed, and what did not

Most published commentary gets this wrong, which is an opportunity.

**No new visa route was created.** CETA locks in existing routes — Standard Visitor and the five Global Business Mobility routes. The UK's own explainer concedes that in most areas "what we have locked in is below what India can currently access."

Treaty numbers are **binding floors**, not the current offer:

| Category | CETA bound floor | Actual current UK rule |
|---|---|---|
| Business visitors | 90 days per 6 months | Standard Visitor, up to 6 months |
| Intra-corporate transferees | at least 3 years | 5 years in any 6-year period; 9 for high earners; min £48,500 |
| Graduate trainees | covered | 12 months, min £25,410 |
| Investors (UK Expansion Worker) | 1 year | 1 year, renewable to 2-year maximum; min £48,500 |
| Contractual service suppliers | 12 months in any 24, 33 sub-sectors | Service Supplier visa, 6 or 12 months |
| Independent professionals | 12 months in any 24, 16 sub-sectors | newly expanded access |

**Genuine gains:** the UK agreed **not to impose numerical restrictions or an Economic Needs Test**; **independent professionals (self-employed)** gained access they did not have; and **1,800 places/year** are ring-fenced for Indian chefs de cuisine, yoga teachers and classical musicians.

**Unchanged:** border control and the points-based system; sponsorship processes and criteria (amendable at any time by the Home Office); salary thresholds; and all fees — IHS £1,035/adult/year, Immigration Skills Charge £1,000/year (£364 small sponsor), CoS £525.

**Client costs:** sponsor licence £611 small/charitable or £1,682 medium/large; priority handling £750 for 10 working days; standard decisions under 8 weeks, with possible UKVI site visit.

**Two traps to flag to clients at the outset:**
1. **The UK Expansion Worker route caps at 2 years with no settlement path.** The switch to Skilled Worker sponsorship must be planned at incorporation, not at month 20.
2. The gov.uk explainer contradicts itself on whether GBM time counts toward settlement. Treat as unresolved; check the Immigration Rules before advising.
3. Skilled Worker general salary threshold has risen steeply: £26,200 → £38,700 (April 2024) → **£41,700 (July 2025)**. HMRC is also hardening its view that visa costs are taxable benefits.

## 5. The corridor: how big, what shape

This is the section that should drive commercial decisions.

### Scale and trajectory
Grant Thornton's *India Meets Britain Tracker* (13th edition, 2026), which counts UK-incorporated companies with Indian ownership:

| Metric | 2024 | 2025 | 2026 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indian-owned UK companies | 971 | 1,197 | **1,912** (+59.7%) |
| Combined turnover | £68.1bn | £72.1bn | **£105.8bn** |
| Employees | 118,430 | 126,720 | **203,549** |

122 of the additional companies came from improved address analysis rather than genuine new entities, so organic growth is **+49.5%** — still the largest single-year rise in the Tracker's history.

CII/Glass.ai counts **3,000+** Indian-owned UK entities (+33% YoY) using web-crawl methodology that includes branches and sub-threshold entities. The ~1,100 gap between the two counts is itself useful: it is roughly the population of Indian-linked UK presences too small to appear in filed-accounts databases. **That gap is the boutique client base.**

**DBT inward investment:** India is the **2nd largest source market by project count** in FY2025-26 (93 projects, 12,687 jobs), behind only the US. Project count fell 12% but jobs more than doubled.

**The contrarian data point:** ONS inward FDI stock from India **fell 49.8% to £6.1bn** at end-2024. Reconciled with rising company counts, this says Indian UK entry is **operationally intensive but capital-light** — subsidiaries staffed with people, funded through intercompany trading rather than large equity injections. For an adviser this is good news: many small structuring engagements, not a few megadeals.

### Structure: the subsidiary has won outright
Companies House data on India-incorporated companies holding a UK establishment (a branch under CA 2006 Part 34) shows **313 branches in 2022-23, flat-to-declining for a decade, with just 8 new registrations that year.**

Against ~715 net new Indian-owned UK limited companies in a single year, **the branch is effectively a dead structure in this corridor.** Joint ventures barely feature either; where a partner is needed, the observed instrument is a minority stake, not a JV vehicle.

Do not build a service line around branch registration or JV formation.

### Size: this is a mid-market and below story
Only **66 of 1,912 companies (3.5%)** clear £5m turnover with 10%+ growth. Strip out the 16 largest employers (95,228 of 203,549 jobs; JLR alone is 44,103) and the remaining ~1,896 companies average **57 employees each**.

The decisive evidence is the UK government's own showcase. During the October 2025 Mumbai visit, gov.uk itemised all **64 Indian FDI projects** announced — and **two-thirds were under £10m**, with a **median of roughly £4–5m**. Several of the highest job-creating ones were people-heavy and capital-light (Mastek: 200 jobs for £2m; Percivon: 90 jobs for £1m).

These are exactly the entities that need a UK Ltd, a PAYE scheme, a sponsor licence, a bank account and someone who understands PSC filings — and exactly the entities that cannot afford a Magic Circle firm or a Big Four global mobility team.

### Demand signal
Grant Thornton's International Business Report 2025: **99% of Indian firms already in the UK plan to expand, 69% within a year. ~90% of Indian mid-market firms not yet in the UK intend to establish there.**

### Where they come from and where they land
Indian source states: Maharashtra 20%, Karnataka 12%, Delhi 8.6%, Gujarat 7.1%, Tamil Nadu 6.7%, Telangana 6.5%. **Top 10 states = 78% of outbound activity**, so targeting five metros covers most of the flow.

UK destinations are decentralising fast: **London's share of the fastest-growing cohort fell from 47% to 38% in one year.** The North generated more revenue (£2.60bn) than London (£2.26bn) from a third as many companies. Financial services is the only London-locked sector (86%). New Indian Consulates General opened in **Manchester and Belfast in March 2025**.

### M&A reality check
India→UK outbound deals: 5 (2023), 12–15 (2024), **20 (2025)** at USD 0.9bn. The 2024 value of £3.41bn was **over 80% one transaction** (Bharti's 24.5% of BT for £3.2bn). Excluding that outlier, 2025 volumes rose 13% and values rose 300%.

Twenty disclosed deals a year is thin. The real advisory volume is in sub-threshold acquisitions that never reach a dealtracker. **M&A should not lead the proposition.**

## 6. Which Indian industries to target

Two flows generate different work.

**Flow A — exporters** needing a UK import/distribution entity. High volume, low fee, light touch: incorporation, VAT/EORI, customs, distribution contracts, registered office.

**Flow B — establishers** building UK operations. Lower volume, high fee, recurring: entity, sponsor licence, ICT/Expansion Worker visas, employment contracts, ongoing compliance.

**Flow B is where a boutique with immigration expertise wins.** Flow A competes on price with £50 formation agents.

### Tier 1 — priority targets

**IT / ITeS / software services.** The clearest fit. **TMT has been the largest sector in the Tracker for 13 consecutive years, at 33% of the fastest-growing cohort.** Exports projected to grow 15–20% annually. This sector is the primary DCC beneficiary — the ~900 companies and 75,000 professionals cited are overwhelmingly here. It generates repeat sponsor licence and ICT work, which is recurring revenue rather than one-off fees.

**Engineering goods and auto components.** **Manufacturing and engineering is the #2 sector at 15%.** UK imports USD 193.52bn of engineering goods but sources only USD 4.28bn from India. Zero duty across **1,659 tariff lines (17% of all lines)**, eliminating tariffs up to 18%. Exports projected to **double to over USD 7.5bn by 2029–30** at 12–20% CAGR. Clusters: Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, UP, Maharashtra, Gujarat, Telangana. Note that UK industrial electricity at **£228/MWh vs Germany £110 and France £65** is a first-order objection for any manufacturing entrant — be ready for it.

**Pharmaceuticals and medical devices.** UK imports nearly **USD 30bn** of pharma but under USD 1bn from India. Duties eliminated on surgical instruments, diagnostic equipment, ECG machines, X-ray systems. Post-Brexit reduction in UK reliance on Chinese supply helps. But note **pharma's share of the fast-growth cohort fell from 22% to 14%** on UK generics pricing pressure — the tariff win may not translate into growth as fast as the headline suggests. MHRA registration also needs genuine regulatory counsel, which is outside current capability.

### Tier 2 — high volume, lighter work

- **Textiles and apparel** — the biggest tariff win: zero duty on **1,143 tariff lines**, eliminating India's disadvantage against Bangladesh, Pakistan and Cambodia. UK imports USD 28.8bn; India supplies USD 1.79bn (6.1% share, 4th largest).
- **Gems and jewellery** — USD 1.03bn into a USD 4bn market, **projected to double in 2–3 years**. Clusters: Surat, Mumbai, Jaipur, Ahmedabad, Kolkata, Hyderabad, Thrissur.
- **Leather and footwear** — USD 494m into an USD 8.9bn market; projections exceed **USD 900m**. Clusters: UP, Tamil Nadu, West Bengal, Delhi NCR.
- **Food processing, agri, marine** — the largest proportional gaps in the deal. UK imports USD 50.68bn of processed food but only **USD 309.5m** from India (985 zero-duty lines). Marine: UK imports USD 4.9bn, India supplies **USD 126m**; shrimp tariffs of 4.2–8.5% eliminated. Agriculture: 1,437 zero-duty lines, exports projected **+50% in three years**.
- **Chemicals and plastics** — 1,206 zero-duty lines (12.4%). India exports USD 40bn globally but only ~USD 570–843m to the UK.
- **Retail** — worth watching: jumped from 1% to 5% of the fast-growth cohort, driven by diaspora-facing organic entries like Malabar Gold's four UK stores.

### Tier 3 — watch, don't lead

- **Financial services** — FCA authorisation work is specialist and outside current capability. Also the most volatile sector in the data (9.5% → 5%).
- **Healthcare and nursing** — the nursing MRA due by July 2027 could matter, but care-sector sponsor licences attract intense Home Office scrutiny and frequent revocation. One revoked client licence would be reputationally severe for a small firm.
- **Sports, gaming and entertainment** — small but the fastest-moving deal sector; average deal value rose from £3.2m (2023) to £144m (2024).

## 7. Regulatory reality check — read before marketing anything

**The proposed bundle cannot be delivered lawfully through an unregulated company as currently structured.** Four of the six services trigger mandatory AML supervision *before trading*; one is criminally restricted; and the M&A element raises an FSMA issue not in the original brief.

### 7.1 The "UK-resident director" premise is false

**There is no UK residency requirement for a director of a UK company.** CA 2006 s154 requires at least one director; s155(1) requires at least one *natural person* — not a resident. gov.uk is explicit: "Directors do not have to live in the UK but companies must have a UK registered office address."

Marketing a nominee director as *legally required* would be a false statement about the law, engaging SRA Principles 2, 4 and 5 and Code paragraph 8.8 (publicity must be accurate and not misleading).

There *is* a real constraint behind the myth: UK banks, HMRC registrations and some landlords often want a UK-based person. **Sell the solution to the actual banking problem, described accurately — not a fictional legal requirement.**

### 7.2 Nominee directorship carries full liability and may be uninsurable

English law does not recognise "nominee director" as a lower-liability category. A nominee bears the entire CA 2006 Part 10 duty set: s171 (act within powers), s172 (promote the success of *the company*, not the appointor), **s173 (exercise independent judgment)**, s174 (care and skill), s175 (avoid conflicts), s176, s177. Plus wrongful trading (IA 1986 s214), disqualification (CDDA 1986), and personal exposure for unpaid PAYE/NIC.

Nominee marketing routinely over-reads s173(2). It permits acting under an agreement *the company* properly entered into or that the constitution authorises — it does **not** permit taking routine instructions from a shareholder, and does not displace s172 or s174.

Directing the nominee also risks making the Indian parent a **shadow director** under s251, so the duties attach to it too. Marketing "you keep control, we're just on paper" creates shadow-directorship exposure for the client while leaving the nominee personally liable. Worst of both worlds.

**Insurance:** "rent-a-director" exposure is a standard exclusion or heavy rating factor in the D&O market. **Confirm cover is obtainable at sensible cost before offering this service at all.** It may not be.

**ECCTA has also killed the anonymity rationale:** since 18 November 2025 every director must verify identity and hold a personal code, making nominees traceable across every directorship.

### 7.3 Four of the six services make Fiat Law a TCSP — with a trading prohibition

Money Laundering Regulations 2017 **reg 12(2)** defines a trust or company service provider to include: **(a)** forming a firm; **(b)** acting, *or arranging for another person to act*, as a director or secretary; **(c)** providing a registered office, business address, correspondence or administrative address.

So incorporation, registered office/mailbox, and director services are each independently caught — and *brokering* a third-party nominee is caught too. HMRC guidance confirms mailbox and virtual office services are in scope, and that from a 30 June 2026 update, selling off-the-shelf companies is expressly TCSP activity.

**The critical point:** FIAT LAW UK LTD is not an SRA authorised body, so it is outside SRA supervision entirely and **must obtain its own HMRC registration**. It cannot shelter under Taylor Rose. The SRA's own guidance confirms it does not matter how infrequently TCSP work is done or whether it is ancillary.

Two features make this urgent:
- **Reg 56(1): a person must not act as a TCSP unless registered.** HMRC: "You must not run your business until HMRC has confirmed that your application has been successful." Unlike most sectors, TCSPs cannot trade while the application is processed.
- **Reg 86(1): criminal offence** — up to 3 months on summary conviction, **up to 2 years on indictment**. Civil penalties plus a £2,000 sanction administration charge.

**Costs (HMRC, from 1 December 2025):** £300 non-refundable application; £400 per premises on registration and annually; **£500 per person fit-and-proper test** (TCSP-specific); £40 per person approval check for the accountancy sector.

Registration triggers the full regime: firm-wide risk assessment (reg 18), policies and MLRO (regs 19–21), CDD/EDD (regs 27–35 — EDD likely mandatory for many India-based structures), records (reg 40), training (reg 24), plus POCA SAR obligations.

> **Supervisory risk flag:** the SRA's own guidance warns that offering a wide range of in-scope services makes a provider look like "a one-stop shop for laundering the proceeds of crime." Formation + registered office + nominee director + accounting, sold as a bundle to non-resident owners, is precisely the FATF high-risk profile. Expect intensive supervision, and design the compliance framework accordingly.

### 7.4 ACSP status — a genuine tailwind, but sequenced behind AML

Under ECCTA 2023, an Authorised Corporate Service Provider (CA 2006 ss.1098A–B) **must be a "relevant person" under MLR reg 8** — AML supervision is a statutory precondition. The registrar must verify supervision with the supervisory authority and must refuse anyone not fit and proper.

Timeline as at today:

| Date | Status | Measure |
|---|---|---|
| 4 Mar 2024 | Live | Registered office "appropriate address" (s86); registered email; lawful purpose statement |
| 18 Mar 2025 | Live | ACSP register opened |
| **18 Nov 2025** | **Live** | **IDV a legal requirement** for directors, PSCs, LLP members; 12-month transition ending ~Nov 2026 |
| 1 Feb 2026 | Live | Companies House fee revisions |
| No earlier than Nov 2026 | **Not yet in force** | CA 2006 s1067A — ACSP-only third-party filing |
| 1 Apr 2028 | Future | Software-only accounts filing |

Today an ACSP is **the only third-party route by which one person can verify another's identity for Companies House** — the alternative is the individual doing it themselves via GOV.UK One Login. Overseas companies must confirm all directors verified by the anniversary of their UK establishment registration.

This is real commercial pull: it creates a compliance reason for overseas-owned companies to need a UK-based regulated intermediary, and raises the barrier against unregulated competitors.

**Sequence: HMRC AML registration first, then ACSP.** The reverse is impossible.

> Several commercial commentaries claim the ACSP-only filing rule is already live. **The primary sources do not support that** — s1067A remains uncommenced. Verify before relying either way.

### 7.5 Registered office: PO boxes are banned

CA 2006 **s86** (in force 4 March 2024) requires an "appropriate address" — one where a delivered document "would be expected to come to the attention of a person acting on behalf of the company" **and** where "delivery of documents is capable of being recorded by the obtaining of an acknowledgement of delivery."

A passive mail-forwarding box fails the second limb. Royal Mail PO Boxes and equivalents are prohibited. Failure is an offence by the company and every officer in default, and Companies House can move the company to a default address and ultimately strike it off.

Third-party service addresses remain permitted. But note the provider's exposure is distinct: it is a TCSP with AML duties over **every** company at the address, must actually receive and acknowledge post, and an address accumulating struck-off companies attracts direct regulatory and law-enforcement attention.

### 7.6 Reserved legal activities — the LSA s15(4) trap

Legal Services Act 2007 s12 and Schedule 2 reserve six activities: rights of audience; conduct of litigation; reserved instrument activities; probate activities; notarial activities; administration of oaths.

**Not reserved:** general legal advice, contract drafting, corporate/commercial advisory, M&A documents, employment advice, tax advice. **But** reserved instrument activities catch preparing a transfer or charge of land, or "any other instrument relating to real or personal estate" — so a market-entry package including a lease, property purchase or debenture crosses the line.

**The trap:** s14 makes carrying on a reserved activity unauthorised an offence (2 years on indictment). s15(2)–(3) provides that an employee carrying on the activity counts, *irrespective of whether the employer is entitled*. s15(4) then provides the employer does not carry it on **unless providing relevant services to the public is part of that employer's business** — and s15(11) extends "employee" to a **manager** of a body.

**Consequence: Fiat Law cannot deliver reserved activities even by using the solicitor's own authorisation.** Supplying such services to the public would be part of Fiat Law's business, so Fiat Law itself would be carrying on the reserved activity unauthorised.

**All reserved work must be contracted with, retained by, billed by and delivered through Taylor Rose**, in Taylor Rose's name, on its terms. Fiat Law may introduce (subject to Code 5.1/5.3) but must not sit in the contractual chain.

### 7.7 Immigration advice — personal criminal exposure

Immigration and Asylum Act 1999 **s84(1)**: no person may provide immigration advice or services unless a qualified person. **s84(2)(ba)** covers those authorised by a designated qualifying regulator, and **s86A(2)(a)** names the Law Society — so **an SRA-regulated solicitor is exempt from separate registration**. Taylor Rose can do immigration work.

**Fiat Law cannot.** It is not authorised by any designated qualifying regulator. Its only routes are registration with the regulator, or reliance on s84(2)(e) (acting under the supervision of a qualified person) — which is fact-sensitive and high-risk as the basis for an entire business line.

**s91(1)**: contravention is an offence — up to 6 months summarily, **2 years on indictment**. **s91(3)–(4)**: where a body corporate offends with the consent, connivance or neglect of a director or manager, **that officer is personally guilty**. A solicitor-director of Fiat Law would be personally exposed.

**The regulator has been renamed: OISC is now the Immigration Advice Authority (IAA).** The statutory office-holder remains the Immigration Services Commissioner and the 1999 Act still says "the Commissioner."

> **Commercially critical open question:** whether corporate **sponsor licence** work constitutes regulated "immigration advice" under s82 (which turns on advice relating to a particular individual) is contested and fact-sensitive; the regulator has historically taken an expansive view. Since sponsor licensing is likely the highest-volume service in the package, **check current IAA guidance directly before structuring this.**
>
> Also verify whether the **Border Security, Asylum and Immigration Act 2025** amended Part V of the 1999 Act.

### 7.8 Accounting

Bookkeeping, accounts preparation, tax return work and payroll are **not reserved** and require no professional body membership — only protected titles ("chartered accountant") are restricted. **But** Fiat Law would be an "accountancy service provider" under MLR reg 11 and needs AML supervision; with no professional body membership, that means HMRC.

**Statutory audit is different and restricted.** A firm must be registered with a Recognised Supervisory Body (ACCA, ICAEW, ICAS or Chartered Accountants Ireland). gov.uk: "It's illegal to audit if you're not a registered auditor and you could be prosecuted." **Exclude audit from any offering.**

### 7.9 M&A advisory — an FSMA problem not in the original brief

Advising on, and arranging deals in, **shares** are regulated activities (Regulated Activities Order 2001 arts 25 and 53). Carrying them on by way of business without authorisation breaches the **general prohibition (FSMA 2000 s19)** — a **criminal offence under s23**, which also renders resulting agreements unenforceable (ss.26–27).

The **art 70 exclusion** (sale of a body corporate) requires the shares to be **50%+ of voting shares** (or the transaction to amount to acquiring day-to-day control), between corporates, partnerships or connected individuals. **Minority-stake, growth-equity and fundraising work falls outside it.**

Critically: **Taylor Rose can use the FSMA Part XX exempt professional firm regime (ss.325–333) because the SRA is a designated professional body. Fiat Law cannot use Part XX at all.** Fundraising work would also engage the financial promotion restriction (s21).

**Take specialist FSMA advice before offering M&A advisory through the unregulated entity.**

### 7.10 SRA separate-business rules

Fiat Law meets the SRA Glossary definition of a **"separate business"**. The operative obligations:

- **5.1(a)** — clients must be told of any financial or other interest in the referral; **5.1(c)** — fee-sharing agreements in writing.
- **5.3** — referral to, or division of a client's matter with, a separate business only **where the client has given informed consent**.
- **5.4** — must not be a manager, employee, member or interest holder of a business whose name includes "solicitors" **or which "describes its work in a way that suggests it is a solicitors' firm"**, unless authorised.
- **8.10** — clients must understand whether and how services are regulated, including **(c) not representing any separate business as SRA-regulated**.
- **8.11** — clients must understand the regulatory protections available.

The SRA **Code for Firms para 7.1** applies 5.1–5.3 and 8.6–8.11 to **Taylor Rose itself**. This is not only the individual's problem; Taylor Rose's COLP has firm-level exposure, and consultant agreements at ABS platforms almost always require disclosure and approval of outside business interests. **Square this with Taylor Rose in writing.**

> **Highest-probability enforcement exposure in the whole structure:** the name. "FIAT **LAW** UK LTD", marketing immigration and corporate legal services, owned by a practising solicitor, with no SRA number displayed, sits squarely in 5.4 territory. Consider also Solicitors Act 1974 s21 and LSA 2007 s17. **This needs independent regulatory counsel before launch.**

### 7.11 Professional indemnity and the privilege gap

**Taylor Rose's SRA policy will not cover Fiat Law work.** The Minimum Terms insuring clause is doubly limited: liability must arise "from private legal practice" *and* be "in connection with the insured firm's practice." The Glossary defines private legal practice as services "in an authorised body." Work contracted with and billed by Fiat Law fails both limbs.

> MTC clause 1.3(b) extends "insured" to service, administration, trustee or nominee companies owned by the firm or its principals. **Do not rely on it** — Fiat Law is a trading company owned by a consultant, and even if 1.3(b) applied, the insuring clause still requires the liability to arise from private legal practice. **Get written confirmation from Taylor Rose's broker and insurer, and check whether the arrangement must be disclosed as a material fact** — non-disclosure of a connected unregulated trading entity is itself a risk to the firm's cover.

Gaps to plug: standalone commercial PI for Fiat Law (far narrower than SRA minimum terms — no unlimited defence costs, no mandatory 6-year run-off); D&O for any directorship service (often excluded); accountants' PI.

**The disclosure that matters most to Indian clients:** clients of Fiat Law get none of the SRA Compensation Fund, Legal Ombudsman jurisdiction, minimum-terms PII, client account protection — and, per ***Prudential plc v Special Commissioner of Income Tax* [2013] UKSC 1, legal professional privilege will generally not attach to advice from a non-lawyer entity.** For a client buying "legal services" on a market entry, loss of privilege is material and often decisive. It is also a strong argument for routing substantive advisory through Taylor Rose.

### 7.12 Context: Taylor Rose's regulatory record

Taylor Rose Limited (SRA 623604) is fully authorised with no restrictions. However, the SRA register records a **fine of £160,059 plus £1,350 costs on 23 March 2026** for Accounts Rules breaches — the client account had not been fully reconciled every five weeks since April 2022, plus failures to report breaches and return client money promptly. A compliance plan completed in August 2025.

This does not affect the solicitor's standing, but it is discoverable by any Indian corporate doing diligence. **Prepare the answer rather than improvising it.**

## 8. Positioning recommendation

### The problem with the stated positioning

"Legal, incorporation, director, mailbox, M&A and accounting for companies entering the UK" describes what **Grant Thornton, BDO, Blick Rothenberg, Menzies, TMF, Vistra and Sovereign** already sell, most with established India desks and hundreds of staff. A solo consultant cannot win a breadth comparison, and the mailbox/formation end competes with £50 formation agents.

Grant Thornton in particular publishes the definitive corridor research (*India Meets Britain*), which means it owns the thought-leadership position by default.

### What is actually defensible

The differentiated asset is not the bundle. It is **a genuinely SRA-regulated immigration solicitor, admitted 2007, with ~18 years of business immigration experience, at the exact moment a trade agreement made staffing a UK subsidiary roughly 23% cheaper for five years.**

The Big Four India desks lead with tax and accounting and treat immigration as a referral. Formation agents cannot give immigration advice at all. That is the gap.

### Recommended shape: lead with mobility, attach the rest

Position as *"the UK landing team for Indian companies — immigration-led."* The client journey:

1. **Diagnostic** — can you use the DCC, which route fits, what does it cost over five years? Fixed fee. This is the wedge, and it is a question accounting firms answer badly.
2. **Entity** — incorporate, registered office, IDV compliance, **bank introduction** (see below).
3. **Sponsor licence** — the highest-value regulated step and the core competence.
4. **Visas** — ICT/Expansion Worker for the initial team, with the 2-year cliff planned from day one.
5. **Ongoing** — compliance, right-to-work, confirmation statements, payroll/accounting via a partner.
6. **Later** — M&A, subject to the FSMA analysis above.

Steps 1, 3 and 4 are the moat. Steps 2 and 5 make the client sticky. Step 6 should not be led with.

### The single best differentiator available

**Bank account opening is the #1 practical blocker** — typically **6–8 weeks**, with KYC on the Indian parent and its beneficial owners. Grant Thornton's own Tracker names it. Banks in practice want a UK-based person, an in-person ID check and a physical trading address, which is what drives the mistaken belief that a UK-resident director is legally required.

A boutique that can (a) correct the legal misconception honestly, (b) provide genuine banking introductions, and (c) solve the underlying substance problem, delivers disproportionate value at the exact moment of maximum client anxiety. **This is more defensible than any nominee product.**

Other high-value corrections worth building content around:
- **NETP VAT registration is from the first supply, not £90,000** — a non-established taxable person has a nil threshold. Indian parents selling into the UK before incorporating routinely get this wrong.
- **PSC compliance in layered Indian holding structures** — failure to respond to a PSC notice within one month carries up to 2 years' imprisonment.
- **The Expansion Worker 2-year cliff with no settlement path.**

### Sequencing

1. **Fix the regulatory foundation first.** HMRC AML registration (remember: no trading in TCSP services before approval), then ACSP registration, PII for the unregulated entity, SRA-compliant disclosure, written agreement with Taylor Rose's COLP, and independent regulatory review of the name. Nothing else can be marketed safely until this is done.
2. **Rebuild the website.** It currently has a page title of "Janeevan", a meta description of "Jon", a typo'd postcode ("1G2 7HU"), dead social links, a placeholder email button resolving to `user@domain.ext`, and no SRA number. Last updated October 2024. It cannot support a corporate proposition and actively creates 5.4/8.10 exposure.
3. **Publish CETA content now.** Three weeks post-entry-into-force is the highest-intent search moment this corridor will see for years. An accurate explainer on the DCC (60 months, not 36) and what it does to staffing costs would out-rank most of what exists, because most of it is wrong.
4. **Pick two Tier 1 sectors and go deep.** IT/ITeS plus engineering is the natural pair — they are the #1 and #2 sectors in the corridor and both are immigration-heavy.
5. **Build referral relationships in India** — chambers of commerce, Indian CA firms and state export promotion councils in Maharashtra, Karnataka, Delhi, Gujarat and Tamil Nadu, which together account for over half the outbound flow. Most UK entrants to this market never do this.
6. **Consider non-London.** London's share is falling, the North over-indexes on revenue, and new consulates in Manchester and Belfast are leading indicators. There is less competition outside the M25.

### Honest caveats

- The market is crowded, and UKIBC, DBT and London & Partners give parts of this away free, compressing willingness to pay at the low end. Don't compete there.
- Growth multiples are large but absolute bases are small. India supplies USD 126m of a USD 4.9bn UK marine market — the fee pool for advisers is not proportional to the percentage growth.
- Tariff benefits require satisfying **rules of origin** and holding valid proof of origin. Grant Thornton's Head of Customs flags this as a live risk. It is a competence to acquire if serving Flow A.
- Most Flow A exporters will use a formation agent and an accountant, not a solicitor. Do not size the business on total Indian export growth.
- The DCC's 60-month exemption is the strongest card, but it is a *cost* argument. Cost arguments attract price-sensitive buyers. Pair it with the compliance-risk argument (PSC criminal liability, sponsor licence revocation, IDV deadlines) to justify professional fees.

## 9. Items requiring verification before relying on them

1. Whether **CA 2006 s1067A** (ACSP-only filing) has been commenced since June 2026 — primary sources say no; several commentaries say yes.
2. The **ACSP registration fee** — appears to be nil, not in the Companies House fee schedule; a £55 figure has been reported elsewhere.
3. Whether the **Border Security, Asylum and Immigration Act 2025** amended IAA 1999 Part V registration or exemption provisions.
4. **Whether corporate sponsor-licence work is "immigration advice"** under IAA 1999 s82 — commercially the most important open question in this document.
5. **RAO art 70 paragraphs (4)–(6)** — whether the sale-of-body-corporate exclusion extends to the advising and arranging articles for the intended M&A activity.
6. **MTC clause 1.3(b)** and insurer disclosure — get this in writing.
7. Whether a **consultant** (contract for services) falls within "own, manage or are employed by an authorised body" in the SRA separate-business definition. It should on "actively participate"/"connected with", but confirm.
8. **S.I. 2025/1037**, amending CA 2006 s1098C (ACSP required information) — not reviewed.
9. CA 2006 Part 42 statutory audit offence section numbers.
10. The Companies House branch series (313 India branches) is from FYE 2022-23, the latest published edition. No later data exists.
