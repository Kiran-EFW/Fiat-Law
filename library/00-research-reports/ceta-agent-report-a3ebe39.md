## 2. TARIFF SCHEDULE HEADLINES

### UK side (what Indian goods get)
- **99% of Indian tariff lines go to zero, covering ~100% of trade value** (India's framing) / **"tariffs being eliminated on 99% of Indian goods"** (UK framing).
- **Critically: there is no phase-in.** UK Impact Assessment, Table 11: *"**All tariff reductions on UK goods imports from India happen at the entry into force of the agreement.**"* Everything Indian exporters gain was available from 15 July 2026.
- Value of UK duty forgone: **£220m/yr** (£180m final goods, £40m intermediates).
- Exclusions from UK liberalisation: **sugar, milled rice, pork, chicken, eggs.**

### India side (what UK goods get)
- **89.5–90% of Indian tariff lines** conceded, covering **91–92% of UK exports**.
- **64% of tariff lines duty-free at EIF** (£1.9bn of 2022 UK exports); rising to **85% of lines / 66% of import value after 10 years**.
- Average Indian tariff on UK goods falls **~15% → ~3%** (Bryant, HCWS262).
- Duty saving for UK exporters: **~£400m/yr at EIF → ~£900m after 10 years**.
- Staging is 5, 7 or 10 years for Make-in-India/PLI-sensitive sectors.

**Independent verification from the Indian gazette** (Notification 29/2026-Customs, 14 July 2026, G.S.R. 628(E), F.No. CBIC-190341/131/2025-TRU): Table I lists **11,106 tariff lines**; I parsed 11,009 machine-readable rows and found **8,005 (72.7%) at 0% BCD + 0% AIDC + 0% Health Cess on day one**. Against India's full ~12,500-line schedule that is roughly 64%, corroborating the UK figure. Gazette copy: `https://a2ztaxcorp.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/India-UK-CETA-Customs-Notification.pdf` (CBIC original: `https://taxinformation.cbic.gov.in/`)

**Textiles & apparel — biggest single winner.** UK MFN 8–12% → 0. UK imports USD 28.8bn of textiles/clothing; India supplies USD 1.79bn (6.1%, 4th largest). Zero duty on **1,143 tariff lines**. UK IA: wearing apparel accounts for **£112m of the £220m total UK duty reduction (51%)**; textiles a further £33m (15%). Modelled UK imports of textiles/apparel/leather **+£2.9bn (+85%)**; partial-equilibrium detail: clothing +£475m (+45%), textiles +£175m (+40%), footwear +£55m (+30%). India was previously the only large supplier paying full MFN while Bangladesh/Pakistan/Sri Lanka enjoyed DCTS preferences — this closes that gap.

**Leather & footwear.** UK MFN up to 16% (8% on the leather footwear lines I verified) → 0. India exported USD 494m to the UK in 2024 into an USD 8.9bn market; PIB projects **>USD 900m**. UK IA duty share: £14m (6%).

**Marine products.** UK MFN up to 21.5% → 0; shrimp specifically was 4.2–8.5% (frozen shrimp lines I checked were at 12%), processed tuna 20% → 0. UK imports USD 4.9bn of marine products; India supplies only **USD 126m** — the largest proportional headroom in the deal. **Caveat: Chapter 3 origin rule is "wholly obtained"** (see §5) — this bites on re-processed imported raw material.

**Food processing & agriculture.** Tariffs up to **70%** eliminated. **985 food-processing lines (10.1%)**; UK imports USD 50.68bn, only USD 309.5m from India. **1,437 agri lines (14.8%)**; PIB projects agri exports up **>50% within 3 years**. Legally significant: **Article 2.13** — *"Originating agricultural goods from a Party shall not be subject to any duties applied by a Party pursuant to a special safeguard taken under the Agreement on Agriculture"* — the UK has given up its WTO agricultural SSG against India.

**Engineering goods & auto components.** Up to **18%** eliminated across **1,659 lines (17%)**. UK imports USD 193.52bn, USD 4.28bn from India; PIB projects **>USD 7.5bn by 2029-30** at 12–20% CAGR. UK IA models machinery & equipment imports +£560m (+45%).

**Chemicals.** Up to 8% → 0 across **1,206 lines (12.4%)**. UK market USD 35.8bn; India supplies ~USD 570–843m. UK IA: £10m duty reduction (5%); PE-modelled imports +£50m (+18%).

**Pharma & medical devices.** Only **56 lines (0.6%)** and mostly already MFN-zero (3004 verified at 0%) — **the pharma "gain" is largely notional on tariffs**. The real movement is the reverse direction (UK medical devices into India). UK imports ~USD 30bn of pharma, <USD 1bn from India.

**Gems & jewellery.** UK MFN 2% (verified 7113 19) → 0. India exports USD 1.03bn into a ~USD 4bn UK import market; PIB projects doubling in 2–3 years. Note India *excluded* gold/jewellery on its own import side.

**Toys & sports goods.** UK MFN ~2% → 0. PIB projects +15%, reaching USD 186.97m by 2030. Modest tariff gain; the value is preference certainty.

**Steel — the exception.** Indian steel gains 0% CETA tariffs but sits *underneath* the UK's new steel trade measure (in force 1 July 2026, 20 categories, 50% out-of-quota tariff, made under the Taxation (Cross-Border Trade) Act 2018 — **not** a WTO safeguard). Resolution before EIF: **~85% of India's steel exports are outside the measure**; 188 tariff lines worth USD 137m (of USD 960m) are covered but given negotiated access. India's Category 1 country quota was raised from **12,405 t to 33,456 t (+170%)** in a late-June revision (verified by diffing the live DBT page against the Internet Archive capture of 14 May 2026); Cat 12B residual 468 t → 4,540 t; Cat 26 residual 10,809 t → 16,327 t; total CSQ 168,029 t; **40% of the Authorised Use Scheme quota reserved for India (~945,000 t)**; 9 commodity codes removed from Category 28. The headline overall quota cut was also softened from 60% (March announcement) to **51%** (June revision).
`https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/uks-steel-trade-measure-from-1-july-2026/uks-steel-trade-measure-from-1-july-2026`

**Services & mobility** (not tariff, but the largest single modelled gain): UK commitments across all 12 major sectors and 137 sub-sectors (>99% of India's export interest); India commits in 108. No Economic Needs Test. Contractual Service Suppliers: 1,800/yr quota, 12 months in any 24 across 33 sub-sectors; Independent Professionals 16 sub-sectors; Intra-Corporate Transferees 3 years; Business Visitors 90 days/6 months. MRAs in nursing, accountancy and architecture to be pursued within 12 months. **DCC**: dual social-security contributions eliminated for up to 60 months, covering >75,000 professionals and ~900 companies; industry-estimated saving **>USD 600m/yr** (UK NI was ~23% of salary). Government procurement: UK opens a **GBP 90bn (USD 122bn)** federal market; India opens USD 114bn.

## 5. CARVE-OUTS, RULES OF ORIGIN AND CONDITIONS FOR CLAIMING PREFERENCE

Governing texts: **Chapter 3** (28 articles) and **Annex 3A** (product-specific rules, HS 2022 basis).
`https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/687e58acae8555bab409a070/uk_india_ceta_chapter_3_rules_of_origin.pdf`
`https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/687e5e378adf4250705c96db/uk_india_ceta_annex3a_product_specific_rules_of_origin.pdf`
India's implementing rules: **Notification 62/2026-Customs (N.T.), 3 July 2026, G.S.R. 560(E)** — the "Customs Tariff (Determination of Origin of Goods under CETA between India and the UK) Rules", effective 15 July 2026, under s.5(1) Customs Tariff Act 1975.

### The "Standard QVC" (Art 3.5 / Annex 3A)
Three alternative thresholds — **40% of ex-works price (build-down), OR 45% of FOB value (build-down), OR 35% of ex-works/FOB (build-up)**. Build-down = (value of good − non-originating materials)/value of good × 100. Build-up = originating materials/value of good × 100.

### Cross-cutting conditions
- **Cumulation (Art 3.8) is BILATERAL ONLY.** No diagonal, no extended, no ASEAN/EU cumulation. Indian exporters cannot count third-country content as originating.
- **Tolerance / de minimis (Art 3.9):** HS Ch 1–3, 5, 6, 10, 14 → **7.5%**; Ch 4, 7–9, 11–13, 15–24 → **12.5%** of value or net weight; Ch 25–98 → **12.5% of value only**. Tolerance does **not** relieve a QVC requirement.
- **Non-qualifying operations (Art 3.7):** 19 listed operations, including — specifically for textiles — "attaching accessory articles such as straps, bands, beads, cords, rings and eyelets; ironing or pressing"; also simple mixing, simple assembly, slaughter, "simple testing, calibration, inspection or certification", and anything done to circumvent the chapter.
- **Non-alteration (Art 3.14):** transit/storage in a non-Party only under customs control, not released to free circulation; permitted operations limited to unloading, reloading, splitting, separation from bulk, storing, labelling, marking, **bottling** (HS 2208 from bulk only) and preservation. Bottling in a non-Party requires transport documents plus a non-manipulation certificate.
- **PROOF OF ORIGIN (Art 3.15) — asymmetric, and this is the operational trap for Indian exporters:**
  > "(a) for importers in the **United Kingdom**, an applicable proof of origin shall be: (i) an **origin declaration** completed by the exporter or producer; (ii) a **certificate of origin** issued by an issuing authority; or (iii) the **importer's knowledge** that the good is originating; and (b) for importers in **India**, an applicable proof of origin shall be **an origin declaration completed by the exporter or producer**."

  So Indian exporters to the UK have three routes (including the legacy eCoO); UK exporters to India have **only one** — a self-declaration, and HMRC registration is mandatory: *"If you have not registered with HMRC your origin declarations will be rejected and your importer will not be able to claim preference."*
- **HMRC document codes:** Origin Declaration = **9001**; Certificate of Origin = **N954**; Importer's Knowledge = **U112**.
- **Validity:** 12 months. Must be **in English**, in writing (electronic acceptable), accompanied by an invoice or commercial document. Single-shipment by default; **UK imports only** may use a multiple-shipment proof for identical goods over ≤12 months. Retrospective proofs must be marked "completed retrospectively" with an explanation.
- **Low-value waiver (Art 3.18): UK imports only**, where customs value ≤ **£1,000**. India granted no reciprocal waiver; to be reviewed at the first Working Group on Rules of Origin.
- **Record keeping (Art 3.24):** importer ≥4 years from importation; producer/exporter 5 years from issuance (in India also the issuing authority).
- **Enforcement:** Art 3.25 verification; **Art 3.26 allows temporary suspension of preferential treatment**; Art 3.28 establishes the Working Group on Rules of Origin. Annexes 3B (declaration template), 3C (certificate template), 3D (authentication framework) and 3E (data protection) govern the mechanics; see also "Modalities of the authentication process for origin declarations" at `https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a561263822fbe6b8245c8ba/modalities-of-the-authentication-process-for-origin-declarations.pdf`.
- **Art 2.6(3):** an importer may claim the lower applied MFN rate where it undercuts the preferential rate — relevant to many Indian goods where UK MFN is already 0–2%.
- **Art 2.7:** "A Party shall not modify or withdraw a concession without the prior agreement of the other Party."
- **Art 2.16:** annual tariff-line-level exchange of preference-utilisation statistics beginning one year after EIF.

### Non-tariff conditions that survive the deal (frequently overlooked)
CETA **does not touch UK SPS requirements**. The Section 42 Agriculture Act 2020 report (Nov 2025, with FSA/FSS Annex A) confirms no changes to UK food/feed law and that risk-based border controls under Assimilated Regulation 2019/1793 continue. Indian products currently subject to enhanced GB border checks include: curry leaves (**50%** physical/identity checks), nutmeg for aflatoxins (50%), groundnuts (50%), sesamum seeds (30%), okra (20%), drumsticks/moringa (20%), guar gum (20%), yardlong beans (20%), Capsicum peppers (20%), cinnamon/cloves/nutmeg/mace/cardamom/anise-cumin-coriander/ginger-turmeric-thyme-bay (10%), betel-leaf foods for Salmonella (10%), and **all Indian rice at 5%** for aflatoxins/ochratoxin A and pesticide residues. Products must enter through designated Border Control Posts. The report is explicit that equivalence recognition requires meeting **UK** MRLs: "A country can have a lower MRL for their domestic market however if they want to export to GB they must meet our MRLs."
`https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6920697525317fc65a5cfdb6/report-persuant-to-section-42-of-the-agriculture-act-2020.pdf`

## 6. CBAM — NO CLAUSE, NO CARVE-OUT, NO REBALANCING MECHANISM

### The finding
**CETA contains no CBAM provision, no carbon-leakage provision and no rebalancing clause.** This was verified by full-text extraction of **67 of the 68** legal-text PDFs published on gov.uk (the one failure being India's numeric tariff-schedule appendix `annex2a_a`, which cannot contain operative language):

| Search term | Hits across all 30 chapters, all annexes, all 12 side letters |
|---|---|
| "CBAM" | **0** |
| "rebalanc" / "re-balanc" | **0** |
| "leakage" | **0** |
| "border adjust" | **0** |
| "carbon" | **3**, all irrelevant (hydrofluorocarbons, Art 21.7; "carbon storage" as a forest ecosystem service, Art 21.12; "low-carbon technologies" in Ch 17 SOE cooperation) |

The draft explanatory memorandum has 0 CBAM hits and exactly 1 "rebalanc" hit — referring solely to bilateral safeguards. There is no CBAM side letter (the 12 published side letters are DCC ×2, IP 1–4 ×2, and Crown Dependencies ×2).

### Why India has no legal remedy inside CETA
Four provisions close off any cause of action:

1. **Art 28.4 (Taxation Measures)** — decisive. "Taxes" expressly exclude customs duties, service-related fees, AD/CVD and safeguard duties; and "Nothing in this Agreement shall apply to indirect taxes, **except for**: (a) Article 2.4 (National Treatment), including GATT Article III… and (b) Article 2.9 (Temporary Admission)." The UK legislated CBAM as a **tax** in Finance Act 2026, administered by HMRC — so the only surviving discipline is National Treatment/GATT III, which CBAM is engineered to satisfy by mirroring the UK ETS price faced by domestic producers.
2. **Art 29.4** — dispute settlement is **violation-only**. No non-violation nullification-or-impairment route, which is precisely what would be needed against a lawful measure that erodes concessions.
3. **Art 21.21** — "Neither Party shall have recourse to dispute settlement under Chapter 29… for any matter arising under this Chapter." The entire Environment chapter, including Art 21.5's CBDR recital, is unenforceable.
4. **Art 2.7** — India cannot lawfully self-help "rebalance" its concessions.

**Art 4.12 (Compensation)** is the only compensation/suspension mechanism and is confined to Section D bilateral safeguards (and bars suspension in the first two years). **Art 30.5** provides only a general ministerial review within 5 years, then every 5 years — no CBAM trigger. **Art 30.7** allows termination on 6 months' notice.

### What actually happened
The rebalancing clause **existed in the negotiating draft and was dropped from the signed text.** Indian Express, 6 May 2025, quoting an Indian official: "The 'rebalancing mechanism' article has been inserted into the 'general exceptions' chapter of the negotiating text… This would enable India to claim compensation for its losses and ensure the UK does not raise a dispute against India at the WTO."
`https://indianexpress.com/article/business/trade-deal-stalled-over-uk-carbon-tax-india-proposes-plan-for-rebalancing-9984730/`

**Piyush Goyal, 26 July 2025:** "Currently there is no CBAM in effect. So it cannot be addressed in the FTA. But India is a sovereign country, and if our export interests are hurt, we will react and retaliate, or 'rebalance'." The same report states plainly: *"the rebalancing measure is **not part of the legal text**."* India registered its concern by **note verbale** — an extra-treaty diplomatic instrument.
`https://indianexpress.com/article/business/economy/uk-deal-gold-standard-ftas-will-retaliate-if-uk-cbam-hurts-piyush-goyal-10151846/`

**Commerce Secretary Rajesh Agrawal, 14 July 2026** (day before EIF): "CBAM is a regulation which is under work. It has yet not come to fruition. Both sides are engaged in discussing CBAM regulation." No formal mechanism was announced.

**Flag as false:** multiple outlets (Economic Times topic pages, universalinstitutions.com, and a GTRI comment reported 25 May 2026 claiming the FTA "includes provisions for consultations and possible corrective measures") assert the deal contains a CBAM rebalancing mechanism. It does not.

### UK CBAM parameters (confirmed)
- **Starts 1 January 2027.** Five sectors: **aluminium, cement, fertiliser, hydrogen, iron & steel.** *Glass and ceramics were dropped* — any source listing seven sectors is out of date.
- **Direct emissions only**; indirect/Scope 2 removed at Budget 2025 (26 Nov 2025). ⚠️ No restoration date is published on gov.uk; the widely-cited "2029 at the earliest" appears in an earlier HMT factsheet and should be treated as indicative.
- Registration threshold **£50,000** (raised from £10,000); year-one registration deadline 31 January 2028.
- One rate per sector, published **quarterly**, built from the UK ETS auction clearing price × a free-allocation adjustment using 2019/2022/2023 ETS baselines. **An illustrative rate is promised for Autumn 2026 — none published yet.**
- **Carbon Price Relief** available against a "qualifying carbon pricing scheme", requiring an accredited-verifier form (5% materiality).
- First accounting period 1 Jan–31 Dec 2027, return and payment due **31 May 2028**; quarterly thereafter. Legislation: Finance Act 2026 plus three SIs laid 13 July 2026.
- Exchequer yield: 2026-27 +£30m; 2027-28 +£140m; 2028-29 +£180m; 2029-30 +£175m; 2030-31 +£155m.
- **CBAM Policy Summary, updated 16 July 2026 — one day after CETA entered into force:** *"There are currently **no international arrangements or agreements** in place"* and *"There are currently **no jurisdictions exempt** from CBAM."* The only statutory exemption route is **ETS linkage** — which India structurally cannot use, because India's CCTS is intensity-based rather than absolute-cap and is therefore not linkable.

### Exposure
**GTRI estimate: USD 775 million** (not GBP) of Indian exports to the UK exposed — iron & steel, aluminium, fertiliser, cement — facing an effective **14–24%** charge. Commerce Secretary Agrawal independently corroborated the 14–24% band but clarified it applies "**on full phase-out of free allowances**" — a terminal-state figure, not a 2027 figure. India→UK iron & steel: ~USD 839–897m (FY26); ~0.14 Mt, only 2.1% of India's steel exports. Indian steel carbon intensity ~2.5 tCO2/t vs ~1.85 global average.

**The offset arithmetic:** UK MFN on Indian steel was ~10–12%; CETA takes it to zero; mature CBAM at 14–24% more than cancels that gain on the covered basket. The exposed basket is ~5.8% of India's USD 13.4bn goods exports to the UK — a serious problem for two sectors, not a systemic threat to CETA's value.

**Stacking is explicit.** DBT implementation notification, updated 22 July 2026: "The UK CBAM… is **separate to** the steel trade measure… will apply from 1 January 2027 **alongside** the steel trade measure… and **in addition to** current trade remedy measures." From 1 Jan 2027 Indian steel therefore faces, cumulatively: the TRQ, a 50% out-of-quota tariff, CBAM, and any AD/CVD — with CETA's zero tariff sitting underneath all four.

**The asymmetry worth flagging:** the EU is on track for full mutual CBAM exemption via UK–EU ETS linkage (agreed in principle May 2025, negotiations opened January 2026, ⚠️ **not concluded as of mid-2026 — secondary sourcing, re-verify**), while India — with a comprehensive FTA in force — has no exemption, no side letter, no carve-out, no CBAM working group and no CBAM committee anywhere in the treaty.
