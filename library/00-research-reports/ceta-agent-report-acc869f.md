## 1. LEGAL SERVICES IN CETA — CONFIRMED EXCLUDED (India side), FULLY SCHEDULED (UK side)

I read the primary treaty text. This is asymmetric, not mutual.

**India's Annex 8B schedule** (https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/687f6a9477a3acd9f4d0e1cd/uk-india-ceta-annex-8b-schedule-of-india.pdf, 44pp): Section II "Sector Specific Commitments → 1. Business Services → A. Professional Services" begins at **(a) Accounting and Book Keeping (CPC 862\*\*)** and runs (b) taxation advisory, (d) architectural, (e) engineering, (f) integrated engineering, (g) urban planning, (h) medical & dental, (i) veterinary, (j) midwives/nurses. **There is no legal services sub-sector at all.** The only two pages in the entire schedule containing the string "legal" are p.3 (horizontal "Legal Entity" commitment) and p.17, which carves legal work *out*: "(c) Management consulting services **excluding all services relating to legal consultancy** (CPC 86501\*\*, 86502\*\*, 86503\*\*, 86505\*\*, 86506\*\*, 86509\*\*)". India's taxation-advisory entry likewise excludes legal advisory and legal representation on tax matters.

**UK's Annex 8B schedule** (https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/687f6be828f29c99778a743a/uk-india-ceta-annex-8b-schedule-of-united-kingdom.pdf, pp.3–4) does schedule legal services, split in two:
- **1.A.a).1 Home country law, other foreign law and international law (part of CPC 861)** — Modes 1, 2, 3: **None** (i.e. fully open, bound) in both market access and national treatment columns; Mode 4 unbound except as per horizontal.
- **1.A.a).2 Domestic UK law** (excluding notarial/public-function services) — Mode 1 None except commercial presence may be required for reserved activities and residency for immigration work; Mode 2 None; Mode 3 "None, except non-discriminatory legal form requirements apply."
- Footnote 3 defines legal services to include advisory, representation, **arbitration and conciliation/mediation**, documentation and certification (excludes bailiffs and patent/trade mark attorneys). Footnote 4: "other foreign law" requires qualification in the relevant foreign jurisdiction.

**MFN (Annex 8C)** (https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6891ca2f25ba7325501b0a1f/uk-india-ceta-annex-8c-most-favoured-nation-treatment-sectoral-coverage.pdf): the UK's list includes "(a) Legal services (CPC 861)"; India's list (items (a)–(qq)) does **not**, and item (u) again excludes legal consultancy from management consulting. So the UK owes India MFN on legal services; India owes the UK nothing.

**Mode 4 (Annex 10A)**: the UK's schedule (https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/687f5e68ae8555bab409a0e5/uk-india-ceta-annex-10aii-...-schedule-of-the-united-kingdom.pdf) lists under **both** Contractual Service Suppliers and Independent Professionals "(a) legal advisory services in respect of home country law, other foreign law and public international law (part of CPC 861)", stay of up to 12 months in any 24, subject to a university degree and "the professional qualifications legally required to exercise that activity in the United Kingdom". India's Mode 4 schedule (https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/687f5e0a88c74f0fd15c9742/...schedule-of-india.pdf) contains **no legal services** and repeats the legal-consultancy carve-outs.

**Annex 8A (Professional Services)** (https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/687e52d992957f2ec567c608/uk-india-ceta-annex-8a-professional-services.pdf) never mentions lawyers or legal services. It is a pure framework: Art 8A.4(1) requires each Party within **12 months of entry into force** to identify professions of mutual interest and encourage relevant bodies to negotiate MRAs, "with a view to concluding those negotiations within **36 months**"; Art 8A.7 creates a Working Group on Professional Services meeting first 12 months after EIF. Nothing self-executing; nothing lawyer-specific.

**Chapter 8 body** contains no legal-services carve-out or review clause. **There is no legal-services side letter or MoU among CETA's associated documents** — the published associated instruments are the Double Contributions Convention, four IP-related side letters and Crown Dependencies territorial-extension letters only.

**Is there a commitment to keep negotiating?** Only outside the treaty, and only politically: the **India-UK Vision 2035** (25 July 2025) commits both sides to "**Deepen ties between the Indian and UK legal professions by affirming commitment to closer bilateral co-operation via the established UK India Legal Profession Committee**" (https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/india-uk-vision-2035/india-uk-vision-2035). The Lords IAC (para 167) expressly notes the omission "runs counter to" that. In evidence, **UK Chief Negotiator Kate Thornley** described a "principled fundamental challenge" because "**India does not consider legal services a tradeable service**" (IAC report para 169, Q55).

⚠️ **Source-of-confusion flag:** commerce.gov.in's CETA factsheet (https://www.commerce.gov.in/files/2026-04/India-UK-CETA%20%282%29.pdf) states "The agreement provides greater market access in IT and IT-enabled services, financial and **legal** services, professional and educational services…". This is not supported by India's schedules; on the text it can only describe the *UK's* opening to Indian suppliers. The PIB 15 July 2026 backgrounder contains **zero** occurrences of "legal".

## 2. BAR COUNCIL OF INDIA RULES

### Statutory/judicial backdrop
Advocates Act 1961 ss.29/33 (only enrolled advocates may practise), s.24 proviso (reciprocity-based enrolment of foreign nationals), s.47 (reciprocity), Chapter V (discipline). *Bar Council of India v. A.K. Balaji*, (2018) 5 SCC 379, **13 March 2018**: foreign firms/lawyers cannot set up offices or practise in India; fly-in fly-out (FIFO) permitted only on a "casual" case-to-case basis; participation in international commercial arbitration permitted subject to a code of conduct; BCI/Central Government empowered to frame rules. ⚠️ The Lords IAC report (para 162) dates this to 2019 — that appears to be an error in the report.

### BCI Rules for Registration and Regulation of Foreign Lawyers and Foreign Law Firms in India, 2022 — **gazetted 10 March 2023**
Permitted registration to practise **foreign law, international law and international arbitration on non-litigious matters only**; strictly no Indian law, no appearance before Indian courts/tribunals/statutory authorities; **reciprocity** required; FIFO already capped at "60 days in any period of 12 months" (Entry Rule 3(1)) but tied to a "procurement in a foreign country" condition; arbitration participation limited (Entry Rules 8(2)(ii)–(iii)) to disputes where parties have a foreign address/principal office, "foreign law may or may not be involved". **Fees were prohibitive: US$50,000 registration for a firm plus a US$40,000 "guarantee amount"** (Covington client alert, 31 March 2023: https://www.cov.com/-/media/files/corporate/publications/2023/03/bar-council-of-indias-rules-for-registration-and-regulation-of-foreign-lawyers-and-foreign-law-firms-in-india-2023.pdf). The Law Society described the 2023 rules as remaining "in limbo".

### Amendment gazetted **13 May 2025**
Notified as amendments to the 2022 Rules (Gazette of India, 13 May 2025; BCI press note reproduced at https://taxguru.in/corporate-law/bci-amends-foreign-lawyer-rules-india.html; Legal Bites summary: https://www.legalbites.in/categories/law-library/legal-gk/bci-notifies-final-rules-for-foreign-lawyers-registration-in-india-gazette-dated-13-may-2025-1142028). Key features (from Majmudar & Partners, 23 May 2025, https://www.majmudarindia.com/wp-content/uploads/2025-05-23-BCI-Rules-Entry-of-Foreign-Law-Firms-in-India.pdf, and Vahura's rule-by-rule table, https://vahura.com/media/docs/entry-of-foreign-law-firms-lawyers-in-india/BCI-Rules-for-registration-and-regulation-of-foreign-lawyers-2025.pdf):

- **Registration categories:** (i) foreign lawyer (individual) — Form A; (ii) foreign law firm — Form B; (iii) **Indian–Foreign Law Firm (IFLF)** — Indian advocates/Indian firms that wish to practise foreign law/international law; plus (iv) FIFO declaration — Form C.
- **Indian-qualified lawyers are squarely covered.** The IFLF category and the expanded definition of "practice of foreign law" bring Indian advocates and Indian firms doing foreign-law/international work — and Indian lawyers working within foreign structures — inside the registration net. Registered foreign lawyers/firms may engage Indian advocates, but those advocates cannot practise Indian law or appear before courts while so engaged. **This is the provision that later underpinned BCI's enforcement action against Indian firms with global alliances.**
- **Permitted scope:** non-litigious foreign law, international law, and international commercial arbitration; expressly no Indian law, no court/tribunal/statutory-authority appearances.
- **FIFO:** **60 days in aggregate in any 12-month period**, Form C declaration and fee required, no office, advice on foreign/international law only. ⚠️ **Discrepancy:** the gazette text as reproduced by taxguru and summarised by Majmudar says "60 days **in aggregate** within any 12-month period … counted from the first day of arrival, regardless of any interim departure and re-entry"; Vahura's own comparison table and several Bar & Bench/LiveLaw reports say "60 **consecutive** calendar days". I could not resolve this against the official gazette (see access failures below).
- **Fees and deposits (2025):** registration US$25,000 (firm) / US$15,000 (individual), "**or the equivalent fee in the primary jurisdiction, whichever is higher**"; additional jurisdiction US$10,000 / US$5,000; **refundable security deposit US$30,000 (firm) / US$10,000 (individual)** (+US$5,000 / US$2,500 per extra jurisdiction); 5-yearly renewal US$15,000 / US$8,000; **FIFO declaration US$6,000 (firm) / US$3,000 (individual) per declaration**. Note this is a *reduction* for firms versus 2023 (US$50,000 + US$40,000) but a new, heavy burden on individual counsel. Barbara Mills KC gave the Lords the individual figures: "a deposit of $10,000, a registration fee of $15,000 and if you fly in and fly out a fee of $3,000" (IAC para 165).
- **Reciprocity** retained as a gating condition, with home-regulator/embassy certification.
- **Disciplinary jurisdiction:** registrants submit to BCI's disciplinary jurisdiction and BCI's professional conduct rules; BCI may suspend/cancel registration.
- Reuters framed the amendment as tightening, not loosening: "India issues stricter rules for foreign lawyers flying in for client work", 15 May 2025 (https://www.reuters.com/world/india/india-issues-stricter-rules-foreign-lawyers-flying-client-work-2025-05-15/).

### ⚠️ CORRECTION TO THE PREMISE ON "END OF JULY"
The Bar & Bench exclusive reporting that BCI would notify amended rules "by end July" to open the market to UK lawyers is dated **28 June 2024**, not 2025. That deadline was **missed**; the amendment did not arrive until 13 May 2025. Separately, on 23 July 2025 BCI chairman Manan Kumar Mishra told a dinner for Law Society president Richard Atkinson that liberalisation was "a commitment, not a compromise" (Bar & Bench, 25 July 2025) — that article's body was paywalled and I could not verify beyond the headline/lede.

### 2025 enforcement wave
- **18 June 2025 BCI press release** (https://images.assettype.com/barandbench/2025-06-18/6zfky91o/BCI_Press_Release.pdf) attacking SILF and invoking s.24 and *A.K. Balaji*; announced a **committee chaired by Cyril Shroff** (with Ajay Bahl, Suhail Nathani, Sandip Bhagat, Mahesh Agarwal, Amit Kapur) to examine concerns about the rules (Bar & Bench, 14 June 2025).
- **5 August 2025:** BCI press release + show-cause notices naming **Dentons Link Legal** and **CMS IndusLaw**, alleging unauthorised practice of foreign law without registration (Majmudar, 1 September 2025: https://www.majmudarindia.com/wp-content/uploads/2025-09-01-BCI-Issues-Show-Cause-Notice-to-Indian-Law-Firms.pdf).
- **21 August 2025:** Delhi High Court (CJ Devendra Kumar Upadhyaya and Tushar Rao Gedela) in *Atul Sharma v. BCI* restrained BCI from passing any final order on the show-cause notice and criticised the pre-judgment naming (https://www.livelaw.in/high-court/delhi-high-court/dentons-link-legal-tie-up-foreign-law-firms-rules-violation-301518; https://www.thehindu.com/news/cities/Delhi/delhi-hc-restrains-bci-from-final-action-against-dentons-link-legal-over-foreign-tie-up/article69961130.ece). On **28 August 2025** the Court recorded BCI's undertaking to withdraw and reissue the release.
- **21 October 2025:** BCI withdrew and reissued the release, now targeting **Swiss Vereins, strategic alliances, exclusive referral arrangements and joint branding**, invoking Rule 36 of the Standards of Professional Conduct and *A.K. Balaji* ("foreign firms cannot do indirectly what they cannot do directly"), and asserting that in India-seated arbitrations foreign lawyers cannot appear or cross-examine where evidence is on oath under s.19(3) of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1996, except on a discrete foreign-law issue (Bar & Bench, 21 Oct 2025; law.com, 22 Oct 2025).
- Separate writ *Narendra Sharma v. BCI* in the Delhi High Court challenging the rules: adjourned to 14 August 2025, then to **4 November 2025**. Majmudar: "The enforceability of the Revised BCI Rules remains under question until the pending litigation in the Delhi High Court is decided."

### **2026 — THE MATERIAL NEW DEVELOPMENT: DRAFT ADVOCATES (AMENDMENT) BILL, 2026**
Published by BCI for consultation on **18 July 2026**, three days after CETA entered into force; comments closed **31 July 2026, 3pm** (SCC Online, 21 July 2026: https://www.scconline.com/blog/post/2026/07/21/bci-draft-advocates-amendment-bill-2026/; Bar & Bench, 21 July 2026: https://www.barandbench.com/news/law-firms-in-india-must-register-with-bar-councils-bci-proposes-changes-to-advocates-act; LiveLaw 21 July 2026 (paywalled): https://www.livelaw.in/top-stories/bci-proposes-amendments-to-advocates-act-542202).

- New **Chapter IIIA (ss.28A–28B)**: first-ever statutory recognition and **mandatory registration of Indian law firms** (partnership/LLP/company of advocates); multi-state firms register with BCI as well as State Bar Councils.
- Statutory definitions inserted for "law firm", "**foreign lawyer**", "**foreign law firm**" and "**fly-in fly-out**" — moving the 2023/2025 rules from subordinate BCI regulation into the Advocates Act itself.
- BCI empowered to register/renew/suspend/cancel foreign lawyer and firm registrations and to permit FIFO — **but rules on foreign-lawyer registration and FIFO would require prior Central Government approval**.
- **Express statutory bar**: foreign lawyers and firms cannot appear before Indian courts, tribunals, authorities or forums reserved to enrolled advocates, cannot practise Indian law, cannot undertake Indian litigation, cannot claim advocates' rights. **Sole exception: appearance before an arbitral tribunal in an international commercial arbitration where foreign law governs the substance of the dispute.**
- **The s.24 proviso permitting enrolment of foreign citizens on reciprocity is proposed for deletion** — foreign nationals would no longer be enrollable as Indian advocates at all.
- Reciprocity retained as a bar/retaliation mechanism against countries that restrict Indian lawyers.
- BCI's own language: "**The doors of India would be opened up for foreign law firms in phased manner only i.e. in a way which could suit the Indian Law Firms**" and "Bar Council of India is not going to do anything which could hamper/adversely affect the interest of our Indian Law Firms in any manner."
- BCI disclosed that the **Cyril Shroff committee has submitted its report** and that BCI "would consider the report before proceeding further" — i.e. **as of 4 August 2026 the Shroff recommendations have not been adopted and the report has not been published**.

UK reaction to this, from the Law Society Gazette, **24 July 2026**, "Fresh blow to India legal market liberalisation hopes" (https://www.lawgazette.co.uk/news/fresh-blow-to-india-legal-market-liberalisation-hopes/5127490.article): "Liberalisation of India's mammoth legal market appears more distant than ever… The new amendments appear to constitute a retreat on amendments to Bar Council regulations published last year." The Gazette adds that "**earlier this year the government insisted that legal services would form part of future trade talks with India**".

### Have any UK/foreign firms actually registered?
**No confirmed registration of any foreign law firm or foreign lawyer could be verified.** Findings:
- The BCI maintains no publicly accessible register I could reach (see access failures).
- The **Bar Council of England and Wales told the Lords IAC (written evidence, October 2025**, https://www.barcouncil.org.uk/static/b8a712c9-d217-4e6d-83015157ee44e4f8/Bar-Council-submission-Inquiry-into-UK-India-FTA-Final.pdf) that against a historic baseline of ~100 India-seated arbitrations a year, "**only one such arbitration taking advantage of the new rules has taken place in the last six months or so**", and that it "required two references to the regulator, the BCI, mid-arbitration hearing"; disclosure requirements were "probably in breach of the terms of the arbitration agreement and/or client confidentiality"; and "high fees and refundable deposits have rendered the regime unworkable for self-employed counsel". ## 3. RECIPROCAL DIRECTION — UK MARKET ACCESS FOR INDIAN LAWYERS

**Confirmed: the UK is already essentially open, and CETA adds almost nothing substantive.**

- Practising **foreign law in England and Wales is not a reserved legal activity**. Under the Legal Services Act 2007 only six activities are reserved (rights of audience, conduct of litigation, reserved instrument activities, probate, notarial, administration of oaths). An Indian advocate can advise on Indian law, international law and transactional matters in London without any UK authorisation, and an Indian firm can open a London office without SRA authorisation provided it does not carry on reserved activities.
- **Registered Foreign Lawyer (RFL)** regime under Courts and Legal Services Act 1990 s.89 and Sch.14 allows a foreign lawyer to be a partner/manager/owner of an SRA-authorised firm. **India ("advocate") is on the SRA's list of approved professions/jurisdictions for RFL registration.** ⚠️ I verified this earlier in the research but did not re-confirm the exact SRA page URL in this session — cite as SRA guidance, "Registered foreign lawyers", sra.org.uk.
- The **Registered European Lawyer (REL)** route is essentially closed post-1 January 2021 (retained only for a defined group of Swiss lawyers) and is irrelevant to Indian lawyers.
- **Requalification:** the **SQE** (which replaced the QLTS in September 2021) is open to Indian-qualified lawyers, with the SRA able to grant exemptions from SQE1/SQE2 based on existing qualification and experience. There is no nationality or residence requirement to be admitted as a solicitor. Entry to the Bar of England and Wales for foreign-qualified lawyers runs through the BSB's transferring-qualified-lawyers route.
- **What CETA adds:** (i) it **binds** the existing openness — UK Annex 8B legal services entries are "None" for Modes 1–3 on foreign/international law, so the UK cannot backslide; (ii) **MFN** on CPC 861 in Annex 8C, so India automatically gets any better legal-services terms the UK grants a third country; (iii) a **Mode 4 route** — Indian legal advisers on home/foreign/public international law qualify as Contractual Service Suppliers and Independent Professionals with up to 12 months' stay in any 24-month period. The 1,800/year quota applies only to chefs, classical musicians and yoga instructors, not lawyers. Practically, (iii) still requires "the professional qualifications legally required to exercise that activity in the United Kingdom", which for unreserved foreign-law advice is nil.
- **MRPQ:** Annex 8A is a **framework only** — no lawyer-specific content, no MRA, no obligation to conclude anything. The IAC (paras 172–176) confirms this reading and records evidence from John Cooke that the 36-month target is "quite an ambitious target" given how "remarkably unsuccessful" comparable UK-EU TCA MRPQ negotiations have been.

**Net:** the asymmetry is total. Indian lawyers and firms can operate in London today with no CETA needed; UK lawyers still cannot establish in India, and their FIFO/arbitration access rests on contested subordinate rules that BCI is now proposing to harden into statute.

## 4. REACTIONS

**Law Society of England and Wales** — press release "UK-India trade deal misses the target on legal services market access", **6 May 2025** (https://www.lawsociety.org.uk/contact-or-visit-us/press-office/press-releases/uk-india-trade-deal-misses-the-target-on-legal-services-market-access). President **Richard Atkinson**: "We welcome the UK government's achievement as a marker of much improved trade arrangements with India but are disappointed to see that the UK-India FTA has been agreed without reference to legal services. This is a missed opportunity for a significant breakthrough in terms of market access for lawyers in both India and the UK. Legal services is an enabling sector… **India is one of the last large jurisdictions in the world in which the establishment of foreign lawyers is not possible, meaning a lot of India-related legal work currently takes place outside of India**… We would welcome renewed discussions to progress on legal services market access, especially in view of the regulations announced by the Bar Council of India in March 2023 for foreign lawyers and law firms which **currently remain in limbo**." Context figures given: UK legal services contribute £57.8bn annually and support ~526,000 jobs.

**Bar Council of England and Wales** — "Bar Council disappointed India trade deal does not extend to legal services", **7 May 2025** (https://www.barcouncil.org.uk/resource/bar-council-disappointed-india-trade-deal-does-not-extend-to-legal-services.html). Chair **Barbara Mills KC**: "While the UK trade agreement with India is overall positive news for the country, we are really disappointed that it does not extend to legal services. **Our aim is not — and has never been — to compete with Indian law firms but rather to complement and work alongside them to enhance the international arbitration landscape.** This was a **once-in-a-lifetime opportunity**… We'll continue to work with the UK government to consider next steps and through our memorandum of understanding with the Bar Council of India" (tripartite BCEW/BCI/LSEW MoU signed June 2023).

**Bar Council written evidence to the Lords, October 2025** (link above) adds: exclusion is "disappointing"; limitations persist for both "India's ambition to become a global arbitration hub, and the UK's to expand its professional-services exports"; "**India's liberalisation remains administrative rather than legislative, and the BCI retains discretion to alter or suspend the rules**"; "The introduction of regulatory supervision for non-court-based dispute resolution, such as arbitration, appears to be unique to India through the world."

**House of Lords International Agreements Committee, HL Paper 253, 3 February 2026** (full text obtained via White Rose mirror: https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/237486/1/Scrutiny%20of%20international.pdf; official: https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/ld5901/ldselect/ldintagr/253/25309.htm):
- Summary (p.3): "in order to get the Agreement over the line, a number of notable UK interests were omitted… the Government did not conclude a bilateral investment treaty, or… arrangements in relation to legal services, new market access for financial services, or final arrangements for mutual recognition of professional qualifications… **Legal services, meanwhile, were entirely omitted, which we consider a missed opportunity.**"
- Para 145/conclusion 22: "The lack of new market access for financial services and legal services access is particularly disappointing."
- Para 166: "**The scope of legal matters that UK lawyers are permitted to practice under the new BCI rules is also currently unclear**… issues around temporary practices have arisen, and the Law Society told us that clarification is still required."
- Para 168 (Mills): "the big ask is the establishment of firms", with a more "modest" ask "to be able to fly in and fly out to give advice and act in arbitration".
- Para 170 (conclusion 26): "We note the absence of legal services from the Agreement. We view this as a missed opportunity…"
- **Para 171 (recommendation 27): "We recommend that the Government takes further steps in this area… As initial steps, the Government should clarify the fly-in, fly-out rules and engage with India on lower, more proportionate arbitration fees."**
- Para 193: the omission of legal services and a BIT is attributed to "the geopolitical imperative to deliver an FTA".

**Government response (mid-2026):** the IAC's new chair **Lord Dominic Johnson of Lainston** wrote that the Government's response to the report was "**informationally thin and lacking in substance**", listing among five deficiencies "**ongoing uncertainty around legal services provisions**" (IAC LinkedIn post, ~June 2026, https://www.linkedin.com/posts/international-agreements-committee_the-governments-response-to-our-report-activity-7461023098931208192-K3pz). A Grand Committee take-note debate on CETA was held on **4 March 2026** (Hansard ref A47D1EF1-5F8E-4598-AFBB-E39DD68ADB75).

**Indian side:** BCI's public posture has hardened rather than softened — see the July 2026 draft Bill language above. Indian firm reaction to the May 2025 rules was broadly positive/relieved (Bar & Bench, 14 May 2025, "Indian law firms react to amended BCI Rules"); SILF has been the main domestic opponent of liberalisation and was publicly attacked by BCI in June 2025.

## UNCERTAINTY / UNRESOLVED FLAGS

1. **No confirmed name of any BCI-registered foreign lawyer or foreign law firm.** BCI publishes no reachable register; the Bar Council of E&W's "one arbitration in six months" evidence strongly implies near-zero uptake, but I cannot state a registration count.
2. **BCI's own website is inaccessible to automated retrieval** — barcouncilofindia.org serves a 661-byte JS shell; its `/server/api/` endpoints require authentication; web.archive.org is blocked to this tool. **All rule-text detail is therefore from secondary reproductions** (taxguru, Majmudar, Vahura, Legal Bites, Covington), not the gazette PDF. The late-2025/2026 BCI notification referencing "submission of complete Form-C declarations" was never retrieved.
3. **FIFO 60 days: "aggregate" vs "consecutive"** — sources conflict (see §2). Unresolved.
4. **The Cyril Shroff committee report has been submitted but not published**; BCI says it "would consider" it. A legallyindia forum thread headline suggesting the report recommends foreign firms be "debarred" was not verified. Reported FIFO recommendations of 60 vs 90 days conflict within a single Bar & Bench article.
5. **Delhi High Court status unknown after 4 November 2025** (*Narendra Sharma v. BCI*) and after the four-week counter-affidavit direction in *Atul Sharma v. BCI*. No 2026 reporting located. The interim restraint on BCI deciding the Dentons show-cause notice was still in place as of October 2025.
6. **Outcome of the 31 July 2026 consultation on the Draft Advocates (Amendment) Bill, 2026 is unknown** (closed four days ago). The Bill has no statutory force unless enacted by Parliament and brought into force by the Central Government. A predecessor **Draft Advocates (Amendment) Bill, 2025** (Government-issued, February 2025) was withdrawn after nationwide bar protests — a plausible precedent for this one too.
7. The Lords IAC report dates *A.K. Balaji* to 2019; the Supreme Court judgment is **13 March 2018**.
