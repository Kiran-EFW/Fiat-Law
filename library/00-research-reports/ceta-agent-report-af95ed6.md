# INDIA → UK CORPORATE MARKET ENTRY CORRIDOR
### Evidence base as at 4 August 2026

**Note on data hierarchy.** Everything marked **[HARD]** is a published statistic with a year and a traceable source. Everything marked **[COMMENTARY]** is qualitative, practitioner, or press assertion. Two figures marked **[DERIVED]** are my own calculations from published tables — the method is shown.

The anchor dataset throughout is Grant Thornton's **India meets Britain Tracker**, now in its 13th edition (2026). Its methodology: Bureau van Dijk + Companies House data on **UK-incorporated limited companies** with Indian ownership, using accounts filed up to 31 March 2025. It explicitly **excludes UK branches** of Indian companies — a fact that turns out to matter enormously for section 4.

For a boutique adviser this is *good* news: the flow is many small structuring engagements, not a handful of megadeals.

## 4. TYPICAL ENTRY STRUCTURES — the subsidiary/branch question

This is the section where I found something not published anywhere as commentary. No source directly answers "do Indian companies prefer subsidiaries or branches?" — so I built the answer from two datasets that have never been paired.

**[HARD] — Companies House, Companies Register Activities, Table B1: overseas companies with a registered UK establishment, by country of incorporation.** Source: https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/companies-register-activities-statistical-release-2022-to-2023 (ODS at /tmp/ch.ods)

India-incorporated companies holding a registered UK establishment (i.e. a **branch** under Part 34 Companies Act 2006):

| FYE | Count |
|---|---|
| 2013-14 | 311 |
| 2014-15 | 333 |
| 2015-16 | 322 |
| 2016-17 | 322 |
| 2017-18 | 321 |
| 2018-19 | 320 |
| 2019-20 | 324 |
| 2020-21 | 320 |
| 2021-22 | 318 |
| **2022-23** | **313** |

**New India branch registrations in 2022-23: 8.**

This is the latest edition of that series — I confirmed no 2023-24, 2024-25 or 2025-26 release exists.

**[DERIVED] The structural conclusion.** Against 1,912 Indian-owned UK limited companies in 2026:
- **Stock ratio ≈ 6:1 in favour of the subsidiary** (1,912 Ltd vs 313 branches).
- **Flow ratio is not close.** Roughly **715 net new UK limited companies in a single year** versus **8 new branch registrations** in the last measured year. On flow the branch is essentially a dead structure for this corridor.
- The branch count has been **flat to declining for a full decade** while the subsidiary count has nearly doubled since 2024 alone.

**Why [COMMENTARY, but well-supported]:** the UK establishment regime requires filing the *parent's* accounts (in some cases translated) at Companies House, offers no limited-liability ring-fence, creates a UK permanent establishment for tax without the flexibility of a separate legal person, and is poorly understood by UK banks and counterparties. The Ltd subsidiary is cheaper to run, cleaner for KYC, cleaner for contracting, and — as section 7 shows — much easier to bank.

**Observed entry patterns from named cases [HARD as to facts, from the 2025 and 2026 Trackers]:**

- **Organic wholly-owned subsidiary, then bolt-ons** — the dominant mid-market pattern. *Zydus*: incorporated Zydus Pharmaceuticals UK Ltd, entered organically in 2023, **EBITDA-positive in about 13 months**, then acquired LiqMeds (2023), Comfort Click via Zydus Wellness (Sept 2025), and a French medtech.
- **Acquisition-led entry** — *Delaplex* bought Blueberry Systems, running delivery teams in Birmingham and Gloucester, targeting ~200 staff, and is explicitly using the UK as a bridge to North America. *HCLTech* acquired Axon Group plc (Dec 2008) and now runs the UK as a **structuring hub for onward overseas acquisitions**, hosting European finance and HR functions.
- **Acquisition then holding-company conversion** — *UPL* entered in the mid-1990s by acquiring MTM Agrochemical; the UK entity is now a central holding company channelling funding to European affiliates, with **~60% of UK-manufactured formulations supplying Europe**.
- **Listing plus acquisitions** — *Tenon* listed on AIM in 2008, acquired Office & General (2015) and Elite Cleaning (2017), later delisted.
- **Purely organic diaspora retail** — *Malabar Gold & Diamonds*: four stores, all organic (Green Street London 2023, Leicester 2024, Southall and Birmingham 2025).
- **Client-pull incorporation** — *Sastra Global* incorporated a Manchester subsidiary in 2023 immediately after winning a UK client. This is the archetypal boutique-firm instruction.
- **Hybrid** — *Wipro* operates "through its subsidiaries **and branch**", 4,000+ UK staff, plus a separately FCA-regulated entity (Wipro Financial Outsourcing Services Ltd). One of very few explicit branch users.

**Joint ventures are conspicuously rare.** Across two full Tracker editions and the CII material, essentially no mid-market Indian UK entry is described as a JV. Where a partner is needed, the observed instrument is a minority stake acquisition (GMR's 49% of Hampshire County Cricket Club; Bharti's 24.5% of BT) rather than a JV vehicle. CII describes structures only qualitatively as "subsidiaries, branches, joint ventures and other corporate structures" and publishes no counts.

**[COMMENTARY, BusinessWorld]:** Indian businesses are "increasingly using Britain as a strategic operating base rather than simply a sales market, **leveraging UK subsidiaries to serve European and global operations**." This matches the UPL and HCLTech holding-structure evidence and is the single most commercially relevant structural trend — it implies genuine substance requirements, not brass plates.

## 7. PAIN POINTS

### 7a. Survey data [HARD]

**Grant Thornton International Business Report 2024** — fieldwork April–June 2024, published 23 Sept 2024. Sample: ~3,500 senior executives across 31 economies, including **166 Indian mid-sized businesses** (defined as USD 50m–2bn revenue) and 301 UK mid-sized businesses (£15m–1bn).
Source: https://www.grantthornton.co.uk/insights/uk-and-india-trade-businesses-see-benefits-and-potential-barriers/

Indian mid-market barriers to UK entry:
- **High cost of doing business — 52%**
- **High cost of regulatory compliance — 48%**
- **Immigration policies and visas — 42%**

Also: **89% rank the UK as their preferred international destination**; 92% said an FTA would prompt them to explore the market; 90% claimed solid understanding of likely FTA provisions (vs 72% of UK firms).

*Note: the press-release version of this survey ranks the three barriers without percentages; the percentages above come from the fuller GT insight page. Use the GT insight URL when citing figures.*

**IBR 2025 (published October 2025) [HARD]:**
- **99%** of Indian firms already present in the UK plan to expand — **69% within a year**
- **~90%** of Indian firms *without* a UK base intend to establish one

That last figure is the demand signal. Roughly nine in ten Indian mid-market companies not yet in the UK say they intend to come.

**I could not find a UKIBC survey of inbound barriers.** UKIBC's "Doing Business in India" series runs the opposite direction (UK firms entering India). No equivalent inbound instrument appears to exist — which is itself worth noting to the client as a content gap they could occupy.

### 7b. Specific frictions

**Banking — the single biggest practical obstacle [COMMENTARY, but from a guide written specifically for Indian businesses]**
Ecovis UK: bank account opening "sounds simple. Frequently, it is not." KYC/AML on foreign-owned entities means **"the process can take six to eight weeks,"** with documentation demanded on the Indian parent *and* its beneficial owners. Advice is to start before or alongside incorporation and to use bank introductions.
Source: https://www.ecovis.co.uk/news/setting-up-a-uk-subsidiary-for-indian-businesses/

**[HARD, GT 2025 Tracker]:** "strict KYC norms and banking compliance procedures can challenge new Indian businesses establishing operations in the UK, slowing down market entry."

**[COMMENTARY, practitioner-level]:** high-street banks in practice want a **UK-resident director, an in-person ID check, and a physical trading address**. Fintech/EMI accounts approve in 1–10 business days by comparison. This is where a boutique firm with banking introductions creates disproportionate value.

**VAT / HMRC [HARD, gov.uk]**
- Registration threshold is £90,000 — **but a non-established taxable person (NETP), i.e. a business based outside the UK making UK supplies, must register "regardless of taxable turnover."** Effectively a **nil threshold from the first supply**. This trips up Indian parents selling into the UK before incorporating.
  Source: https://www.gov.uk/vat-registration/when-to-register
- Corporation tax notification within **3 months** of the start of the first accounting period.
- PAYE scheme must be live **before the first payday**; register 4–8 weeks ahead.
- Late-filing penalties from £150.

**UK-resident director — the most valuable correction to make [HARD, gov.uk]**
There is **no legal requirement for a UK-resident director.** "Directors do not have to live in the UK but companies must have a UK registered office address." Directors must be 16 or over and not disqualified or an undischarged bankrupt.
Source: https://www.gov.uk/limited-company-formation/appoint-directors-and-company-secretaries

**The UK-resident director requirement is a banking and practical constraint, not a statutory one.** Many Indian clients arrive believing it is law. Getting this distinction right — and then solving the *actual* banking constraint behind it — is a clean differentiator.

**Registered office [HARD, gov.uk]** — must be a physical address in the country of registration and an **"appropriate address"** (someone will be made aware of post arriving; delivery can be confirmed). Royal Mail PO Boxes and equivalents are banned. Non-compliance can lead to strike-off. A registered email address is also now required (not published).
Source: https://www.gov.uk/limited-company-formation/company-address

**PSC register [HARD, gov.uk]** — triggered by >25% of shares or voting rights, power to appoint or remove a majority of directors, or other significant influence. Bands: 25–50%, 50–75%, 75%+. **Failure to respond to a PSC notice within one month, or providing false information, carries up to 2 years' imprisonment and/or a fine**; share and voting restrictions can also be imposed. Indian group structures with layered holding companies routinely get this wrong.
Source: https://www.gov.uk/guidance/people-with-significant-control-pscs

**ECCTA identity verification — the live commercial opening [HARD, gov.uk]**
- A **legal requirement from 18 November 2025**.
- Directors supply a Companies House personal code via the confirmation statement.
- PSCs have a **14-day window** (PSC-only individuals: the first 14 days of their birth month).
- **An overseas company must confirm all its directors have verified by the anniversary of its UK establishment registration.**
- **Authorised Corporate Service Providers** (accountants, solicitors, formation agents) can verify clients and **will eventually be required to file on their behalf**.
Source: https://www.gov.uk/guidance/when-you-need-to-verify-your-identity-for-companies-house

**[HARD, GT 2026 Tracker]:** "Incorporation, disclosure, and reporting requirements through Companies House are more rigorous... For mid-sized Indian businesses entering the UK for the first time, navigating these requirements can add time and cost to market entry."

**Immigration [HARD]**
- Skilled Worker general salary threshold: £26,200 → **£38,700 (April 2024)** → **£41,700 (July 2025)**
- Visa fees £719–£1,639; Immigration Health Surcharge £1,035/yr; **Immigration Skills Charge £364–£1,000 per sponsored worker per year**
- Sponsor licence required, plus a Certificate of Sponsorship per employee
- **HMRC is hardening its view that visa costs (including renewals) are taxable benefits** — GT flags this as "a critical issue for employers with mobile workforces"
- **UK Expansion Worker (Global Business Mobility)**: for businesses that have "not started trading in the UK yet"; **maximum 12 months plus one 12-month extension (2 years total)**; 5 years in any 6-year period across GBM routes; **no route to settlement**
  Source: https://www.gov.uk/uk-expansion-worker-visa

The UK Expansion Worker route's 2-year hard cap with no settlement path is a structural trap: it forces a switch to Skilled Worker sponsorship at exactly the point the UK entity is scaling. Plan for it at incorporation, not at month 20.

**Cost base [HARD]** — corporation tax 25% above £250k profits (up from 19% in April 2023), small profits rate 19% up to £50k · National Living Wage £11.44 → £12.21/hr · employer NICs 13.8% → **15% (April 2025)** · non-dom regime replaced April 2025 with a 4-year FIG regime.

**Energy [HARD]** — UK large industrial electricity **£228/MWh in 2024** vs Germany £110, France £65, Sweden £52. Brent above USD 100 and wholesale gas +75% between late February and March 2026. This is a first-order objection for any manufacturing entrant.

**Public sector [HARD/COMMENTARY]** — **Developed Vetting can take up to a year** and is often required for all project personnel; procurement rules privileging prior UK experience exclude otherwise capable Indian firms.

**Other legal exposure [HARD]** — **No India–UK bilateral investment treaty since India terminated it in March 2017**; a replacement is under negotiation. NSI Act 2021 screening applies to sensitive-sector acquisitions. Supply-chain criminal exposure under the Bribery Act, Modern Slavery Act, ECCTA and Criminal Finances Act, where "adequate procedures" is the only defence. GT's Head of Customs flags **CETA rules-of-origin determination** as a live risk.

**Advisers [COMMENTARY — but the most quotable evidence for the client's pitch]**
Angad Rajain, Global MD of Tenon: *"The partners and advisors that you choose to work with play a massive role in your success. Choose the right advisors, and that journey will make a difference between success and failure."*
GT 2026 Tracker conclusion: *"businesses must seek advice from experts in both countries who understand the regulatory environments, business cultures, and operating realities."*
Malabar Gold reports "scope for simpler market-entry processes." Jaipur Rugs asks for stronger support on hiring, subsidies and estate taxes.

## 8. SME vs LARGE CORPORATE — the section that matters most

The headline numbers make this look like a conglomerate story. **It isn't.** Employment is concentrated; company count and deal flow are not.

**[HARD] Turnover bands of the fastest-growing cohort**

| Band | 2026 (66 cos) | 2025 (74 cos) |
|---|---|---|
| £5m–25m | 28 | 33 |
| £25m–250m | **34** | **39** |
| >£250m | 4 | 2 |

**[DERIVED] Only 66 of 1,912 companies (3.5%)** cleared the Tracker's screen of £5m+ turnover, 10%+ growth and two years of accounts. Even allowing generously for companies that are large but not growing, **the overwhelming majority of the 1,912 sit below £5m turnover.** The Tracker is a survivorship-filtered top slice; the population underneath it is small-company territory.

**Employment concentration [HARD]:** 16 firms account for 95,228 of 203,549 jobs. JLR alone is 44,103 — 21.7% of all Indian-owned UK employment in one company. Strip out the top 16 and the remaining ~1,896 companies average **57 employees each.**

**Median announced project: roughly £4–5m.**

**This is the answer to the brief's question.** When the UK government assembles its single biggest showcase of Indian inward investment, **two-thirds of the projects it can point to are under £10m** — and several of the highest job-creating ones (Mastek: 200 jobs for £2m; Percivon: 90 jobs for £1m) are people-heavy, capital-light service businesses. These are precisely the entities that need a UK Ltd, a PAYE scheme, a sponsor licence, a bank account and a director who understands PSC filings — and precisely the entities that cannot afford a Magic Circle firm or a Big Four global mobility team.

Also from that visit: 29 UK→India investments worth >£3.6bn; a £350m defence/missile deal with 700+ Northern Ireland jobs; Yash Raj Films committing to 3 films and 3,000+ jobs.

## POLICY TAILWIND

**[HARD]** The India–UK **Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement (CETA)** was signed in July 2025 and **entered into force on 15 July 2026** — three weeks ago as at today's date. Alongside it, the **Double Contributions Convention** gives Indian secondees a **3-year UK social security exemption**. 99% of Indian exports get zero-duty access; 130+ services sub-sectors covered; tariff reductions phased from April 2026; bilateral trade target of USD 100bn by 2030.

Supporting institutions: UK–India Technology Security Initiative (July 2024); India–UK Connectivity and Innovation Centre (£24m, 6G and AI-native networks); India–UK Joint Centre for AI; Critical Minerals Processing Guild; India–UK Climate Finance Initiative with an SBI Climate Tech Startup Fund.

**Diaspora [HARD]:** 1.9m+ British Indians, 3.1% of the UK population, the largest ethnic minority — directly relevant to the Malabar-style organic retail entries and to referral-led origination.

## SUMMARY FOR A BOUTIQUE FIRM

1. **The corridor is at an all-time high and accelerating** — 1,912 companies (+60%), 203,549 employees (+61%), and CETA in force three weeks ago. **99% of Indian firms already here plan to expand; ~90% of those not here intend to come.**
2. **The structure is overwhelmingly the UK Ltd subsidiary.** 6:1 on stock, and roughly 715 new subsidiaries against 8 new branch registrations on flow. Branch registrations have been flat for a decade. JVs barely feature.
3. **The volume is mid-market and below.** Two-thirds of the government's own showcase projects were under £10m; median ~£4–5m. Only 3.5% of Indian-owned UK companies clear £5m turnover with growth.
4. **The pain points are exactly the ones a boutique firm solves**: bank account opening (6–8 weeks, the #1 practical blocker), NETP VAT registration from the first supply, the mistaken belief that a UK-resident director is legally required, PSC compliance in layered Indian group structures, ECCTA identity verification since 18 Nov 2025 (with an ACSP filing monopoly coming), and the 2-year cliff on the UK Expansion Worker visa.
5. **Geography is decentralising** — London fell 47% → 38% in one year. Manchester and Belfast now have Indian Consulates General. Financial services is the only London-locked sector (86%).
6. **The two data tensions to handle honestly**: GT's 1,912 vs CII/Glass.ai's 3,000+ (databases vs web-crawl; the ~1,100 gap is the sub-radar population); and DBT's "India is the UK's #2 FDI source market" vs ONS inward FDI stock falling 49.8% to £6.1bn (activity is capital-light and people-heavy, which favours advisory volume over deal volume).
