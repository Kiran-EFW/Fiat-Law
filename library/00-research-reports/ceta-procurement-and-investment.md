## A2. Thresholds — the figures you had seen are WRONG; here are the treaty values

All thresholds are SDR-denominated (Annex 15A Sections A/C).

| | Goods | Services | Construction |
|---|---|---|---|
| **India** (Section A, central govt) | **SDR 450,000** | **SDR 450,000** | **SDR 5,000,000** |
| **UK** (Section A, central govt) | **SDR 130,000** | **SDR 130,000** | **SDR 5,000,000** |
| **UK** (Section C, airports/ports utilities) | **SDR 400,000** | **SDR 400,000** | **SDR 5,000,000** |

Indicative currency equivalents (business.gov.uk, not the treaty):
- India SDR 450,000 ≈ **Rs 5.5 crore / £480,000**; SDR 5m ≈ **Rs 62 crore / £5.3m**
- UK SDR 130,000 ≈ Rs 1.6 crore / **£138,000** — this is almost certainly the source of the "~£100,000" figure you recall.
- The **"Rs 200 million"** figure does not correspond to anything in the treaty.

**Special UK services sub-thresholds** (Annex 15A.ii Section E Note 1): hotel/lodging (CPC 641), food & beverage (642, 643), legal (861), government administration services (91136, 9127), education (92), and recreational/cultural/sporting (96) are covered only above **GBP 663,540** (Section A entities) / **GBP 884,720** (Section C entities) — i.e. a deliberately raised bar in politically sensitive services.

**Threshold floor / ratchet — Art 15.22(6):** a Party can never be required to go below **SDR 130,000 goods, SDR 130,000 services, SDR 5,000,000 construction** for central government entities. So India's SDR 450,000 is above the floor and is a live target for future reduction under the Art 15.22 further-negotiations clause (which is triggered if either Party gives better coverage to a third partner, and includes a specific ratchet against thresholds offered to GPA parties).

**Threshold adjustment:** India must calculate and publish INR values *before* entry into force, and re-notify **not later than two years from the previous notification**, using IMF conversion rates (Annex 15A.i Section H). The UK adjusts **every even-numbered year effective 1 January** (Annex 15A.ii Section H).

## A3. Indian entities covered

- **Section A lists 51 numbered central government entities** (47 Ministries + Department of Administrative Reforms & Public Grievances, Department of Pension & Pensioners' Welfare, Department of Official Language, **Department of Atomic Energy**), plus ~37 named sub-departments (e.g. all six Departments of the Ministry of Finance; Dept of Posts and Dept of Telecommunications; Dept of Pharmaceuticals). business.gov.uk: *"51 central government ministries and departments."*
- **Subordinate entities** procuring independently are covered and treated as separate procuring entities — but footnote 1: *"a 'subordinate entity'… does not include an entity with a separate legal personality."*
- **CPSEs — counterintuitive design.** Note 2: *"All central public sector enterprises ('CPSEs') established or maintained by the central government entities listed in this Section are covered… **except any CPSE exposed to competitive market forces in any of its operations**."* Three-factor test (competitors present; market-based pricing; no legal barrier to market entry). So the **commercially attractive CPSEs (ONGC, IOCL, NTPC, BHEL, SAIL, Coal India etc.) are effectively OUT**, and the covered ones are the monopolistic/statutory ones. The Annex gives an **indicative list of 28 CPSEs determined NOT exposed to competitive forces — i.e. these ARE covered**: Food Corporation of India, National Research Development Corporation, Uranium Corporation of India, NE Handicrafts & Handlooms Dev Corp, Karmayogi Bharat, National Handloom Dev Corp, National Safai Karamcharis F&DC, National SC F&DC, National Backward Classes F&DC, Konkan Railway, Rail Vikas Nigam, Dedicated Freight Corridor Corporation, Indian Railway Finance Corporation, Mumbai Railway Vikas Corporation, BIRAC, Artificial Limbs Manufacturing Corp, National Handicapped F&DC, NEDFi, NE Venture Capital, NEDFi Trustee, National Minorities D&FC, National Industrial Corridor Development Corp, J&K and Ladakh Development Finance Corp, CERSAI, Pipavav Railway Corp, **NHIDCL**, ITPO Service Ltd, NMDC CSR Foundation.
- Footnote 2 expressly excludes **Security Printing and Minting Corporation of India**, its subsidiaries, and Bank Note Paper Mill India Pvt Ltd.
- **Ministry of Railways (entry 35) and Ministry of Road Transport and Highways (36) ARE covered** — the largest procurement lines in the schedule.

**Entities conspicuously ABSENT (hence excluded):** Ministry of Defence, Ministry of Home Affairs, **Ministry of Electronics & Information Technology (MeitY)**, Department of Space, Ministry of Mines, PMO/Cabinet Secretariat. Corroborated by business.gov.uk: *"Defence, Home Affairs, IT and Space are excluded on India's side."* (Note: Department of Atomic Energy IS included — an unusual inclusion.)

## A4. Sub-central — EXCLUDED, and on BOTH sides
- **India, Annex 15A.i Section B "Sub-Central Government Entities: Nil." Section C "All Other Entities: Nil."** No states, no municipalities, no state PSUs.
- **UK, Annex 15A.ii Section B: "Nil."** No English local authorities. (Scottish Ministers, Welsh Ministers and Northern Ireland Ministers are covered but as *Section A* central entries, items 44.x, 48.x, 26.x.)

## A5. "Class-II local supplier" under Make in India — Art 15.4(8)

Art 15.4(7) bans offsets generally. Art 15.4(8) is the carve-back:

> *"Notwithstanding paragraphs 1, 2, and 7, India and its procuring entities shall be entitled to seek, take account of, impose, or enforce the MII Order at any stage of a covered procurement, **provided that India and its procuring entities treat any supplier of the United Kingdom as a 'Class-II local supplier' under the MII Order where: (a) that supplier offers goods or services having a country of origin as the United Kingdom; and (b) the goods or services have equal to or greater than the minimum 'local content' prescribed for Class-II local suppliers in the MII Order** where 'local content' means the amount of value added in the United Kingdom…"*

- MII Order = Public Procurement (Preference to Make in India) Order 2017, No. P-45021/2/2017-PP (BE-II), as revised 16 September 2020.
- **Class-I = ≥50% local (Indian) content; Class-II = ≥20%.** CETA lets UK value-add substitute for Indian value-add *for Class-II purposes only*.
- **Footnote 3: UK value-add and Indian value-add can be combined** — *"this provision shall not prevent a supplier of the United Kingdom from contributing towards the minimum local content in India."*
- Practical effect: a UK supplier with ≥20% UK content escapes the "non-local supplier" tier (which can be excluded entirely from MII-notified procurements, or ranked last in purchase-preference), but **does not reach Class-I**, which retains first call. UK conclusion summary §4.15: *"UK companies will be treated as a class 2 supplier if at least 20% of their product or service is from the UK – granting them the same status that only Indian firms currently enjoy (the Make in India preference will still apply for approved 'class 1' suppliers offering 50% or more of their goods or services from India)."*
- Art 15.4(9): India may amend the MII Order provided UK treatment stays *"at least equivalent."* Art 15.22(7)–(9) contains an MII-specific ratchet: better MII treatment given to another partner triggers consultations with the UK.
- business.gov.uk calls this *"a first for any trade partner."*
- **Reciprocal:** Art 15.4(10)–(11) lets the UK apply its **Social Value** regime, provided Indian suppliers are treated no less favourably, criteria are objectively verifiable, and any self-certification route is equally open to Indian suppliers. (ANI, 9 May 2025: *"UK agreed to non-discriminatory treatment for Indian suppliers under their Social Value regime."*)

## A6. India's MSME 25% set-aside — FULLY PRESERVED
- **Annex 15A.i Section G(1)(b): Chapter 15 shall not apply to "preferences provided under the Public Procurement Policy for Micro and Small Enterprises (MSEs) Order, 2012, as amended from time to time."** Unlimited and open-ended (the "as amended" language means India can expand it).
- That Order reserves **25% of central procurement for MSEs** (incl. 4% SC/ST, 3% women), plus 358 items exclusively for MSEs and a 15% price-preference band.
- GTRI's criticism at the time: near-equal UK access *"could push Indian MSMEs out of the market"* (ANI, 9 May 2025). Structurally, the carve-out means UK suppliers compete for the *non-reserved* portion.
- **UK reciprocal carve-out — Annex 15A.ii Section G Note 4:** *"The provisions of Article 15.19 (Domestic Review Procedures) do not apply to suppliers and service providers of India in contesting the award of contracts to a supplier or service provider, which are SMEs as defined in Section 123 of the Procurement Act 2023, **until such time as the United Kingdom accepts that India no longer operates discriminatory measures in favour of certain domestic small and minority businesses**."* This is a tit-for-tat: Indian bidders lose the right to challenge UK SME awards for as long as India runs the MSE Order.

- **Is the UK offering more than its GPA schedule? No — it is offering LESS in scope, but it is 100% new access for India.** The UK's GPA schedule includes an Annex 2 (sub-central: English local authorities, etc.) and a much fuller Annex 3 utilities annex (water, electricity, urban transport, ports, airports). CETA's Section B is Nil and Section C covers only airports and ports. So CETA ≈ a subset of GPA Annex 1 + a slice of Annex 3. Since **India is not a GPA party**, everything here is genuinely new market access for Indian suppliers. *(This GPA comparison is my analysis from reading both schedules; I did not find a UK government document stating it in those terms — treat as reasoned inference.)*

## A8. Transition / phase-in periods
There is **no phase-in of coverage** — all entities and thresholds bite from 15 July 2026. The time-bound elements are:
1. **GeM compliance: 12 months.** Art 15.4(4) footnote 2: *"India shall ensure that its Government e-Marketplace (GeM) platform complies with this paragraph no later than 12 months after the date of entry into force"* → **by 15 July 2027** for interoperable/generally-available IT systems and tender-integrity mechanisms.
2. **Dispute settlement moratorium: 4 years.** **Art 15.23: *"Neither Party shall have recourse to dispute settlement under Chapter 29 (Dispute Settlement) for any matter arising under this Chapter for a period of four years from the date of entry into force of this Agreement."*** → Chapter 15 is **not state-to-state enforceable until 15 July 2030**. This is the single most important commercial caveat in the chapter.
3. **India permanently exempt from minimum tendering periods.** Art 15.11(2): the 25-day (selective tendering, requests to participate) and 40-day (tender submission) minimums in paras 3–8 apply only to UK entities — *"For greater certainty, paragraphs 3 through 8 shall not apply to procuring entities of India."* India is bound only by the general "sufficient time" obligation in Art 15.11(1) and its own law. **This is a permanent asymmetry, not a transition.**
4. India's INR threshold publication was due *before* entry into force (Section H(1)).
5. Working Group on Government Procurement (Art 15.21) meets at least every two years; the EM lists it as working group #6 of 10. General Review of the whole Agreement within five years.

## A9. Exclusions — consolidated

**India (Annex 15A.i Section G):** (a) essential security interests, arms, ammunition, war materials, national defence/security procurement; (b) MSE Order 2012 preferences; (c) agricultural products for agricultural support and human feeding programmes (incl. food aid); (d) overseas missions and any Indian entity outside India; (e) procurement from another government entity; (f) goods/services procured outside India for consumption outside India; (g) any entity not listed in Section A.

**Chapter 15 itself, Art 15.2(3) — activities not covered (both sides):** acquisition/rental of land, buildings or immovable property; grants, loans, equity infusions, guarantees, fiscal incentives; fiscal agency, depository services, liquidation/management services for regulated financial institutions, public-debt services; public employment contracts; procurement funded by international assistance or by an international organisation under its rules; procurement conducted for the stationing of troops.

**Art 15.3 General Exceptions:** public morals/order/safety; human, animal or plant life or health; IP protection; goods or services of persons with disabilities, philanthropic institutions, or prison labour.

**UK (Annex 15A.ii Section G):** Note 1(c) *"procurement of any goods or services by entities in the field of defence or security"*; broadcasting programme material; agricultural support programmes. Note 2: water/energy/transport/postal sectors. Note 4: the SME domestic-review carve-out (see A6). Plus the Section E/F services and concessions restrictions and the UK Space Agency exclusion.

# B. INVESTMENT

## B1. Is there an investment chapter? — NO
- The gov.uk collection lists **30 chapters and there is no investment chapter**. Nothing on expropriation, fair and equitable treatment, investor-State dispute settlement, or minimum standard of treatment appears anywhere in the chapters I downloaded (Ch.8 Trade in Services, Ch.9 Financial Services, Ch.15, and the associated annexes) — zero hits for "expropriation", "fair and equitable", "investor-State", "ISDS".
- The **Associated Documents** page contains only **12 side letters** (2 on DCC, 8 on IP, 2 on Crown Dependencies). **Nothing on investment, financial services or procurement.**
- Confirmations: JD Supra, "UK and India free trade agreement enters into force" — *"CETA does not include an investment chapter."* Econiti, 25 July 2026 (https://www.econiti.org/daily-news/2026-07-25/2026-07-25-india-uk-ceta-unfinished-gaps/) — *"no dedicated investment chapter,"* one of three items deliberately parked outside the deal alongside pharma patent standards and the UK CBAM; UK investors get *"no treaty-based protections and no investor-state dispute mechanism under CETA."*
- Freshfields, "India's Investment Treaty Landscape in 2026" (https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/risk-and-compliance/indias-investment-treaty-landscape-in-2026-102mmwp): CETA, like the EFTA and Oman deals, *"does not grant rights to investors"* — ISDS excluded; only State-to-State dispute settlement under Chapter 29.
- **What DOES exist is establishment-type liberalisation, but only via GATS-style services scheduling** in Chapter 8 (Trade in Services, Arts 8.1–8.19) and Chapter 9 (Financial Services, Arts 9.1–9.23), through **Mode 3 (commercial presence)** commitments in Annexes 8B and 9A. That is market access and national treatment for service suppliers — **not** investment protection, and not general (non-services) investment.
- **MFN:** Art 8.7 exists but India's MFN is a *positive list* (Annex 8C) of named service sectors — accounting/auditing (862), taxation (863), architectural (8671), urban planning (8674), engineering (8672/8673), medical & dental (9312), veterinary (932), nursing/midwifery/physio (93191), computer hardware installation consultancy (841), software implementation (842), data processing (843), database (844), etc. — with carve-outs for regional economic integration/internal-market agreements, right-of-establishment agreements, legislative approximation, and LDC agreements. **Chapter 9 (Financial Services) contains NO MFN article at all.**

## B4. India's Model BIT — 2016 baseline and the 2026 revision in progress

**2016 Model BIT (baseline, per Freshfields):** enterprise-based definition of "investment"; requires "substantial business activities" in the home State; **five-year exhaustion of local remedies** before arbitration; broad carve-outs (notably taxation); **no general fair and equitable treatment standard**; **no MFN clause**. India issued termination notices to ~68–75 BITs in 2016–17.

**Recent practice drifting off the 2016 template:** India–UAE BIT (2024) cut local remedies to **three years** and covers portfolio investments; India–Kyrgyzstan and India–Uzbekistan retained five years but extended the limitation period from three to six years and added full protection and security for physical assets; India–Brazil ICFT (in force 2025) confers **no** investor rights at all (Ombudsman + inter-State only).

**2026 revision (announced Union Budget 2025-26; confirmed ongoing by MoF on 1 Feb 2026; no draft published):** reported to rest on three pillars —
- **local remedies cut to a minimum of two years** (possibly plus a one-year cooling period),
- **no MFN clause**,
- **taxation fully excluded from ISDS**.
(Vajiram & Ravi, 9 June 2026, sourcing Indian Express: https://vajiramandravi.com/current-affairs/india-revamps-its-bilateral-investment-treaty-model/; also Moneycontrol "India weighs stricter BIT framework, may mandate 2-year local remedy period".)

Note the tension: a "no MFN" model is directly incompatible with the UK's reported MFN demand, which explains the deadlock.

## B5. The 1994 India–UK BIPA — terminated; sunset still running
- Agreement between the UK and India for the Promotion and Protection of Investments, **signed 14 March 1994, in force 6 January 1995** (UK Treaty Series No. 27 (1995), Cm. 2797: https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a7c083ded915d01ba1cab5a/2797.pdf).
- **UNCTAD: status Terminated, date of termination 22 March 2017, "Unilaterally denounced"** (https://investmentpolicy.unctad.org/international-investment-agreements/treaties/bit/1968/india---united-kingdom-bit-1994-). UK Parliament written answer 2021-06-08/12320: *"The BIT was unilaterally terminated in 2017, following notice of termination provided by the Government of India in 2016."*
- It contained **Article 5 (Expropriation)** and **Article 9 (Settlement of Disputes between an Investor and a Host State)** — full ISDS.
- **Article 15 (Duration and Termination), verbatim:** *"This Agreement shall remain in force for a period of ten years. Thereafter it shall continue in force until the expiration of twelve months from the date on which either Contracting Party shall have given written notice of termination to the other. **Provided that in respect of investments made whilst the Agreement is in force, its provisions shall continue in effect with respect to such investments for a period of fifteen years after the date of termination** and without prejudice to the application thereafter of the rules of general international law."* (text at https://edit.wti.org/document/show/31ed512e-f760-42c6-a015-273ef2a324c6)
- **The sunset clause has NOT expired.** UK investments *made before 22 March 2017* remain protected — including ISDS — for 15 years from termination.

> ⚠️ **UNCERTAINTY (4) — the sunset end date.** 22 March 2017 + 15 years = **22 March 2032**. But UK parliamentary written evidence (committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/151617) states: *"The treaty contained a 'sunset clause', guaranteeing its application for 15 years post termination. However, after 2033 British investors in India would remain without any international protection."* That implies a 2018 termination effective date. The discrepancy is probably a counting error, or reflects a different view of when notice took effect (India served notice in 2016; the treaty required 12 months' notice). **Treat 2032 as the likely date but flag the 2033 reading.** Both source pages 403'd; text is from search snippets.
- Critically: the sunset protects **legacy** investments only. **Any UK investment in India made on or after 22 March 2017 has no international treaty protection whatsoever** — no BIT, no CETA investment chapter, no ISDS. Recourse is Indian courts.

## B6. FDI liberalisation commitments in CETA

### Insurance — bound at 74%
- **Annex 9A (India's Financial Services Schedule):** life insurance, non-life insurance, and reinsurance/retrocession, **Mode 3**: *"None, except that commercial presence would be through incorporation of a company with foreign investment not exceeding 74 per cent."* Above 49%: at least 50% of net profit must be retained in a general reserve if solvency is below 1.2× the control level, and at least 50% of directors must be independent. National treatment: a majority of directors, key management personnel, and at least one of Chairperson/MD/CEO must be **Resident Indian Citizens**.
- UK conclusion summary §4.9: *"On foreign direct investment, UK ownership or investment into Indian insurance or banking firms is secured at up to 74% UK owned or invested."*

> ⚠️ **UNCERTAINTY (5) — MOST IMPORTANT SUBSTANTIVE FINDING. India's applied law has moved past its CETA binding, and CETA does not capture it.** The Sabka Bima Sabki Raksha (Amendment of Insurance Laws) Act, 2025 was gazetted 3 February 2026 (most provisions commenced 5 February 2026), and the **FEMA (Non-debt Instruments) (Second Amendment) Rules, 2026, S.O. 2186(E), dated 2 May 2026, permit 100% FDI in insurance companies and intermediaries under the automatic route** (LIC remains capped at 20%), subject to a resident chair-or-CEO condition and IRDAI verification. Aviva became the first foreign insurer to fully own its Indian life JV (buying Dabur's residual 26%) around 9 July 2026. **Because Chapter 9 has no MFN article and no ratchet/standstill, CETA does not lock the 100% regime in — India remains legally free to revert to 74% without breaching CETA.** Sources: Mondaq 1783250; Skadden (Feb 2026); Business Today, 2 May 2026; DPIIT.

### Banking — 74% cap, branches UNBOUND
- **Annex 9A, Mode 3 market access: "(i) Unbound for branches of foreign banks."** Legal form restricted to (a) a **wholly-owned subsidiary** of a foreign bank — subject to economic needs tests, reciprocity, inclusive-banking requirements and RBI prudential norms; or (b) FDI in a private-sector bank subject to a **74% ceiling (49% automatic; beyond 49% with Government/RBI approval)**.
- Further limits: a foreign bank's WOS may hold no more than 5% in an Indian private bank; acquisitions of 5%+ need prior RBI approval; foreign banks cannot acquire a fresh stake taking holdings to 10%+; and new WOS entry can be restricted where foreign banks' capital and reserves exceed 20% of the banking system.
- **National treatment: "(i) Unbound for branches of foreign banks. The UK banks branch(es) shall be treated no less favourably than non-Party foreign bank branches, except that India shall not be prevented from applying measures for prudential reasons…"; "(ii) Unbound for wholly owned subsidiaries."** Also: public sector enterprises may place surplus term deposits only with scheduled commercial banks incorporated in India.
- **So: no new branch access for UK banks. The branch mode is expressly unbound — the only protection is a soft "no less favourable than other foreign banks" undertaking.** This is a notable gap given UK banks' interest.

### Asset management — meaningful, but subsidiarisation required
- Cash/portfolio management, collective investment management, custodial, depository and trust services (**excluding pension fund management**), Mode 3: minimum capitalisation norms and *"Commercial presence would be through incorporation as a company in India."* **National treatment Mode 3: None** — i.e. full NT once incorporated. This is the cleanest of India's financial-services commitments.
- Transferable securities: joint venture with foreign investment not exceeding **74%**, limited to recognised foreign stock-broking companies.
- Financial consultancy Mode 1: unbound except research-analyst services supplied by UK entities that have agreements with SEBI-registered research analysts/entities.
- **Payment systems, Mode 1:** *"Access to respective Fast Payment Systems (FPS) on a reciprocal basis, without a need to get onboarded onto the other payment system, through interlinking of FPS for: (i) QR code-based merchant payments, and (ii) instant fund transfers."* (UPI ↔ Faster Payments interlinking.)
- Chapter 9 also contains a Prudential Exception (9.4), Back-Office Functions, New Financial Services (9.14), Recognition, a bespoke Financial Services Dispute Settlement article (9.19), Credit Rating (9.21) and Electronic Payments (9.22).

### Telecoms — 100% bound, but with heavy security conditionality
- **Annex 8B (India's services schedule), telecommunication services Mode 3:** *"The service supplier should be a company registered in India with maximum foreign investment up to 100 percent. For foreign investment more than 49%, Government approval would be required."*
- Conditions: Chief Officer Technical Network Operations, Chief Security Officer and lawful-interception officials must be Indian citizens; a majority of directors must be Indian; security vetting of foreign Chairman/MD/CEO/CFO; minimum two licences per service area; resale of telephone services not permitted; unbound in respect of Indian government PSUs.
- **This is the one sector where CETA binds India at 100%.**

### Horizontal FDI reservation — the escape hatch
- Annex 8B horizontal section, item (iii) "FDI Policy related": market access and national treatment restrictions in the **Consolidated FDI Policy Circular of 2020 (latest) and FEMA regulations, as revised and amended from time to time**, continue to apply — provided changes *"shall not be applied in a manner so as to nullify or impair the benefits under the terms of a specific commitment."* National-security footnotes recur throughout. Residency of Director: at least one director and one manager of an Indian-incorporated company must be resident in India.
- Single Brand Product Retail, Mode 3: foreign investment up to **51%** only (well below India's applied 100% automatic route) — another example of binding below applied.

### Reciprocal (UK side)
- The UK is already an open regime; its Annex 8B/9A commitments largely bind the status quo. The commercially meaningful reciprocal elements are the **FPS interlinking** commitment (mutual) and Indian banks'/insurers' access to the UK under the existing FCA/PRA authorisation regime, which CETA does not alter. I did not find any UK financial-services concession in CETA that goes beyond its existing applied regime. *(Assessment based on reading Chapter 9 and Annex 9A India; I did not exhaustively parse Annex 9A UK — flag as partially verified.)*
- Bilateral context: PIB, 15 July 2026 — the UK is India's 6th largest inward investor with cumulative equity FDI of ~USD 35bn to September 2024; India's outward stock in the UK ~USD 19bn to March 2024; 971 Indian companies in the UK and 667 British companies in India. DBT factsheet (31 July 2026): UK FDI stock in India £19.1bn at end-2024 (https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a69cc36f23ff62c51c1a7ff/india-trade-and-investment-factsheet-2026-07-31.pdf).

# BOTTOM LINE

**Procurement** is the genuine breakthrough — India's first FTA procurement chapter with a real market-access schedule, 51 central ministries/departments plus ~28 non-commercial CPSEs, thresholds at SDR 450,000 (goods/services) and SDR 5,000,000 (construction), and Class-II Make in India status for UK suppliers at 20% UK content. But it is heavily hedged: no sub-central coverage on either side, Defence/Home/MeitY/Space out, the commercially attractive CPSEs out via the "competitive market forces" test, the 25% MSE set-aside fully preserved and open-ended, India exempt from minimum tendering deadlines, GeM not required to comply until July 2027, and **no dispute-settlement recourse at all until 15 July 2030**.

**Investment** is the conspicuous hole. CETA has no investment chapter, no investment side letter, and no ISDS. UK investors who invested on or after 22 March 2017 have zero international treaty protection in India today. The replacement BIT is deadlocked on taxation carve-out and MFN, with India simultaneously rewriting its Model BIT (2-year local remedies, no MFN, tax excluded). And the headline financial-services numbers — 74% insurance, 74% banking — are already below India's applied law (100% insurance since 2 May 2026) with no MFN or ratchet in Chapter 9 to hold that gain.
