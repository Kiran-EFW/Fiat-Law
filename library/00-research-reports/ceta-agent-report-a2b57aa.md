# UK CBAM × India–UK CETA — Research Findings

**Bottom line:** CETA contains **no** CBAM clause, no carbon-leakage clause, and no rebalancing clause — verified by full-text extraction of 67 of the 68 legal-text PDFs published on gov.uk. India's "rebalancing" is a **unilateral political reservation asserted outside the treaty**, not a treaty right. Separately, the UK steel trade measure dispute **was** resolved in India's favour before entry into force, and I found hard primary-source proof of the quota change.

## Q1 — UK CBAM: confirmed parameters (all primary-sourced)

**Start date: 1 January 2027.** Confirmed at HMT/HMRC consultation outcome, 30 Oct 2024:
`https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/consultation-on-the-introduction-of-a-uk-carbon-border-adjustment-mechanism`

**Sectors — five, not seven:** aluminium, cement, fertiliser, hydrogen, iron & steel.
> "Products from the glass and ceramics sectors **will not be in scope** of the UK CBAM from 2027. The sectoral scope of the UK CBAM will be kept under review beyond 2027."

⚠️ **Glass and ceramics are OUT.** They appeared in the Dec 2023 "Addressing carbon leakage risk" factsheet, which is superseded but still live on gov.uk and still lists seven sectors. Any source citing seven sectors is out of date.

**Registration threshold: £50,000** of CBAM goods (raised from the originally proposed £10,000). Two tests — forward-looking (next 30 days) and backward-looking (preceding 12 months from the first of the month); the earlier trigger date applies. Normally 30 days to register, but for year one the deadline runs to **31 January 2028**.

**Rate mechanics** (CBAM Policy Summary, updated 16 July 2026 —
`https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/carbon-border-adjustment-mechanism-cbam-policy-summary/carbon-border-adjustment-mechanism-cbam-policy-summary`):
- One rate per sector, published **quarterly** from 1 Jan 2027.
- Built from (i) average UK ETS auction clearing price for the quarter, × (ii) a free-allocation adjustment = 1 − (average share of sectoral direct emissions covered by free allowances × reduction factor), using UK ETS baseline data for **2019, 2022 and 2023**.
- Liability = (embodied emissions × sector CBAM rate) − Carbon Price Relief.
- "An illustrative example CBAM rate will be set out in **Autumn 2026**." No published rate yet.

**Direct emissions only.** Indirect (Scope 2) emissions were **removed from scope at Budget 2025 (26 Nov 2025)**, "to reflect continued support for the Energy Intensive Industries Compensation Scheme."
⚠️ A secondary source claimed deferral "to 2029 or later" — gov.uk gives **no** restoration date. Treat the 2029 date as unverified.

**Carbon Price Relief (CPR):** effective carbon price × relevant embodied emissions, capped at the CBAM liability. Requires an HMRC carbon pricing verification form completed by an accredited verifier, and the foreign scheme must be a "qualifying carbon pricing scheme" (government-administered, mandatory, publicly available rules and price). Six-step calculation, adjusted for any compensation/rebate received; FX at HMRC rates for the prior calendar quarter. Verifier materiality threshold: **5%** of the good's emissions intensity.

**Legislation:** primary provisions in **Finance Act 2026** (preparatory provisions in Finance Act 2025). SIs laid **13 July 2026**: CBAM (Administrative Provisions) Regs 2026; CBAM (Calculation of CBAM Rate and Determination of Carbon Price Relief) Regs 2026; CBAM (Transitory Provision) Regs 2026. Draft CBAM (Emissions and Verification) Regs 2026 went out for technical consultation 9 April 2026.

**Accounting periods:** first period 1 Jan–31 Dec 2027, return + payment due **31 May 2028**; quarterly thereafter (Q1 2028 → 31 Jul 2028; Q2 → 29 Sep 2028; Q3 → 30 Nov 2028; Q4 → 28 Feb 2029).

**Out of scope:** UK-origin goods (non-preferential rules of origin), scrap aluminium and iron & steel, non-business imports, returned-goods relief, full-relief temporary admission. Applies across the whole UK **including Northern Ireland**, and to goods entering from Crown Dependencies, Overseas Territories and the UK Continental Shelf.

**Exchequer yield** (Budget 2025 policy paper, 26 Nov 2025 — `https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/introduction-of-carbon-border-adjustment-mechanism/carbon-border-adjustment-mechanism`): 2026-27 +£30m; 2027-28 +£140m; 2028-29 +£180m; 2029-30 +£175m; 2030-31 +£155m.

Collection: `https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/carbon-border-adjustment-mechanism`

## Q2 — Does CETA reference CBAM? **No. Definitively no.**

I downloaded all 68 CETA legal-text PDFs from the gov.uk collection
(`https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/comprehensive-economic-and-trade-agreement-between-the-united-kingdom-of-great-britain-and-northern-ireland-and-india`)
and extracted 67 to text (the single failure was India's numeric tariff-schedule appendix, `annex2a_a`, which cannot contain operative language).

**Search results across the entire corpus — 30 chapters, all annexes, all 12 side letters:**

| Term | Hits |
|---|---|
| "CBAM" | **0** |
| "rebalanc" / "re-balanc" | **0** |
| "leakage" | **0** |
| "border adjust" | **0** |
| "carbon" | **3**, all irrelevant |

The three "carbon" hits: hydrofluorocarbons (Art 21.7); "carbon storage" as a listed forest ecosystem service (Art 21.12); "low-carbon technologies" in Ch 17 SOE cooperation language.

The **draft explanatory memorandum** likewise has 0 CBAM/carbon hits and exactly 1 "rebalanc" hit — referring solely to bilateral safeguards.

### Why India's legal position is weaker than the press coverage implies

Four provisions, read together, close off any CETA cause of action against CBAM:

**Art 28.4 (Taxation Measures)** — the decisive one. It defines:
> "'taxes' and 'taxation measures' do not include: (a) a customs duty; (b) a fee or other charge in connection with importation commensurate with the cost of services rendered; (c) an anti-dumping or countervailing duty; or (d) a safeguard duty"

and then:
> "Nothing in this Agreement shall apply to direct taxes… Nothing in this Agreement shall apply to indirect taxes, **except for**: (a) Article 2.4 (National Treatment – Trade in Goods), including Article III of GATT 1994 as incorporated into this Agreement; and (b) Article 2.9 (Temporary Admission)."

The UK deliberately legislated CBAM as a **tax** (Finance Act 2026, administered by HMRC), not a tariff. It therefore falls inside Art 28.4's carve-out, and the only CETA discipline that survives is National Treatment / GATT III — which CBAM is specifically engineered to satisfy by mirroring the UK ETS price faced by domestic producers.

**Art 29.4 (Scope of dispute settlement)** — covers only a measure "inconsistent with its obligations" or a failure "to carry out its obligations." **Violation complaints only.** There is no non-violation nullification-or-impairment route, which is precisely the route India would need for a measure that is lawful but erodes its concessions.

**Art 21.21 (Non-Application of Dispute Settlement):**
> "Neither Party shall have recourse to dispute settlement under Chapter 29 (Dispute Settlement) for any matter arising under this Chapter"

— the entire Environment chapter, **including Art 21.5 (Climate Change), which recites CBDR**, is unenforceable. India's CBDR argument has no forum inside CETA.

**Art 2.7 (Modification of Concessions):**
> "A Party shall not modify or withdraw a concession without the prior agreement of the other Party."

India cannot lawfully self-help "rebalance" its tariff concessions.

**Art 4.12 (Compensation)** is the *only* compensation/suspension mechanism in the agreement, and it is confined to **Section D bilateral safeguards** — not global safeguards, not taxes, not CBAM. It also bars suspension of concessions in the first two years.

Supporting: **Art 2.8** expressly excludes "charges equivalent to an internal tax… applied consistently with Article III:2 of GATT 1994" from the Art VIII fees discipline. **Art 28.1** incorporates GATT Art XX for Chapters 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 12 and 17, and para 3 confirms Art XX(g) reaches living and non-living exhaustible natural resources — that is a **UK defence**, not an India remedy.

**Review/exit:** Art 30.5 provides a ministerial general review within 5 years of entry into force, then every 5 years — **no CBAM-specific trigger**. Art 30.2 requires written agreement plus 60 days for amendments. Art 30.7 allows termination on 6 months' written notice.

**Side letters:** Art 30.1(2) — "Side letters… shall constitute an integral part of this Agreement **if those side letters explicitly so provide**." The 12 published side letters are: Double Contributions Convention (UK + India pair), IP Side Letters 1–4 (UK + India pairs), and Crown Dependencies territorial extension (pair). **There is no CBAM side letter.**
⚠️ Minor unverified observation: IP Side Letter 4 carries a much later gov.uk media ID (`6a564e05…`) than the July 2025 batch (`6881…`), suggesting it was published in 2026. Not confirmed, and it concerns IP, not CBAM.

### Media claims that do not survive contact with the text — flag these as false

- **Economic Times topic page:** "India-UK FTA includes provisions to counter the UK's CBAM… This 'rebalancing' mechanism aims to shield India from potential disputes at the WTO." — **Not in the text.**
- **universalinstitutions.com:** "India included a 'rebalancing mechanism' in the general exceptions of the FTA draft." — This was true of the *negotiating draft* (see Q3), but **not of the signed agreement**.
- **Ajay Srivastava (GTRI) via ET, 25 May 2026:** the FTA "reportedly includes provisions for consultations and possible corrective measures if future carbon taxes disproportionately impact Indian exports." — **No such provision exists.**

The Indian Express itself got it right on 26 July 2025: *"the rebalancing measure is **not part of the legal text**."*

## Q4 — Exposure estimates

⚠️ **Correction to the brief's premise: the figure is USD 775 million, not GBP.**

**GTRI estimate (May 2025, repeated by PTI 18 June 2026): USD 775 million** of Indian exports to the UK (iron & steel, aluminium, fertiliser, cement) exposed to CBAM, facing an effective tax of **14–24%**. An earlier GTRI iteration of the same $775m figure dates back to Dec 2023, so the number has not been re-estimated against the final five-sector scope — treat it as a stable order-of-magnitude, not a current calculation.

The 14–24% band is corroborated independently by Commerce Secretary Agrawal (ET, 15 July 2026), who described it as "14-24% of the import value **on full phase-out of free allowances** under the ETS" — i.e. it is a terminal-state figure, not a 2027 figure. In 2027 the free-allocation adjustment will hold the effective rate well below that.

**Sector data (note the variance — flag it):**
- India → UK iron & steel and products: **$893.4m** (FY26, Agrawal) / **$897.68m** (ET) / **$839m** (PTI, June 2026). Differences reflect basket definition.
- Total India → UK merchandise exports: **$13.44bn** (FY26). Two-way goods trade $25.12bn; services $35.44bn (2024); stated target $100bn in 3–4 years.
- Steel volume: **~0.14 MT**, only **2.1%** of India's total steel exports.
- Carbon intensity: Indian steel **~2.5 tCO2/t** crude steel vs **~1.85** global average (Deloitte India) — a ~35% penalty that flows directly into CBAM liability.

**The offset arithmetic.** UK MFN tariffs on Indian steel ran up to 10–12%. CETA takes those to zero. CBAM at a mature 14–24% of import value **more than cancels the tariff gain** on the covered basket. Ajay Srivastava (GTRI):
> "By not securing a carve-out or exemption clause on CBAM, India lost a vital opportunity to protect its carbon-intensive exports. From January 2027, the UK can impose carbon taxes on Indian steel and aluminium, even as we grant UK goods duty-free access. That's a serious asymmetry. Expect the same treatment in India's FTA with the EU."

He separately noted CBAM could "far exceed the UK's current average tariff rate of under 2 per cent" — the correct framing, since across the *whole* trade basket the average UK tariff India is escaping is tiny, while CBAM concentrates a large charge on a narrow set of goods.

⚠️ Unverified secondary: Hindu BusinessLine put the CETA advantage for Indian steel exporters at only 7–10% and noted India→UK steel is 0.085% of India's total exports.

**Proportion framing worth keeping:** the $775m exposed basket is ~5.8% of India's $13.4bn goods exports to the UK. CBAM is a serious problem for two sectors, not a systemic threat to CETA's value.

## Q5 — 2026 developments: **India obtained nothing**

The CBAM Policy Summary of **16 July 2026** — one day after CETA entered into force — is unambiguous on both possible relief routes:

Section "International arrangements or agreements":
> "Where appropriate, the government will consider of the use of suitable arrangements or agreements with other jurisdictions. **There are currently no international arrangements or agreements in place.**"

Section "Exemption for goods originating in a jurisdiction with a linked ETS":
> "The UK can exempt goods from CBAM, where either they originate from a country or territory which has entered arrangements with the UK to link their ETS with the UK's scheme, or a country or territory whose ETS is indirectly linked to the UK ETS. **There are currently no jurisdictions exempt from CBAM.**"

**The only statutory exemption route is ETS linkage** — and India structurally cannot access it. India's Carbon Credit Trading Scheme (CCTS) is **intensity-based**, not an absolute-cap system, so it is not linkable to the UK ETS. India's only available relief is Carbon Price Relief against CCTS prices, which are far below UK ETS levels and would offset only a fraction of the liability.

**UK–EU ETS linkage:** agreed in principle at the May 2025 UK–EU summit; formal negotiations opened **January 2026**; **not concluded as of mid-2026**. Once linked, mutual CBAM exemption is expected (the EU Council negotiating mandate reportedly states UK CBAM "would not apply to goods originating in the Union"). Sources here are secondary (cbamguide.com 13 July 2026; UK Trade and Business Commission evidence to Parliament warning of up to £800m/yr EU CBAM costs for UK firms by 2030) — ⚠️ the linkage status should be re-verified before being relied on.

**The asymmetry is the story:** the EU is on track for a full CBAM exemption via ETS linkage; India, a signatory to a comprehensive FTA that entered force on 15 July 2026, has no exemption, no side letter, no carve-out, no CBAM working group, and no CBAM committee anywhere in the treaty text.

India–UK CBAM discussions continue but are informal and diplomatic only (Agrawal, 14 July 2026). ⚠️ Unverified secondary: the India–EU FTA is reportedly addressing CBAM for Indian SMEs. EU CBAM entered its definitive regime on 1 January 2026.

## Q6 — The steel trade measure: resolved, and I can prove how

Primary source: `https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/uks-steel-trade-measure-from-1-july-2026/uks-steel-trade-measure-from-1-july-2026` (DBT, published 2 April 2026, updated 22 July 2026).

**This is not a WTO safeguard extension.** It is a **replacement domestic trade measure** made under the Taxation (Cross-Border Trade) Act 2018 as amended. The old steel safeguard quotas and the 25% additional safeguard duty **ceased to apply from 30 June 2026**. The new measure covers **20 product categories**, with a **50% out-of-quota tariff**, plus a quarterly roll-over of quotas within the year and a review after twelve months. The UK is also raising its bound MFN steel tariff at the WTO to 50%.

### The resolution, verified by archive diff

I compared the live page against the Internet Archive capture of **14 May 2026**. Two changes are decisive:

**1. The headline quota cut was softened from 60% to 51%.**
- 19 March 2026 press release (`https://www.gov.uk/government/news/uk-steel-industry-backed-by-major-new-trade-measure-and-strategy`): "overall quota levels for steel imports will be significantly reduced by **60%**."
- 14 May 2026 archived version: "reducing overall quota volumes by **60%**."
- Current version (22 July 2026): "reducing overall quota volumes by **51%**."

⚠️ This resolves the 60%-vs-51% discrepancy in press coverage: **both numbers were correct, at different times.** Reporting that still says 60% is quoting the March announcement.

**2. India's Category 1 quota was nearly tripled.** Table 3, India's dedicated country quotas (annual, tonnes):

| Category | 14 May 2026 | 22 July 2026 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (hot-rolled flat) | **12,405** | **33,456** | **+170%** |
| 4 (metallic coated sheets) | 125,796 | 125,796 | unchanged |
| 20 (gas pipes) | 8,777 | 8,777 | unchanged |

Order numbers: 058601 (Cat 1), 058605 (Cat 4), 058643 (Cat 20).

This also explains the discrepancy noted in the ET piece of 25 May 2026, where Rajib Maitra of Deloitte India cited 12,405 t for Category 1 — **he was quoting the then-current figure correctly**; it was revised upward in late June.

⚠️ Inferred from row adjacency in the extracted table (moderate confidence, worth a visual check): the EU's Category 1 quota rose from 68,226 to 375,000 t over the same revision, while its Category 4 quota *fell* from 634,773 to 510,273 t — suggesting the June revision was a broad rebalancing across partners, not an India-only concession.

### How it was framed politically

PTI / ETManufacturing, 18 June 2026 (`https://manufacturing.economictimes.indiatimes.com/news/industry/uk-steel-curbs-85-of-indias-exports-exempt-rest-protected-through-mix-of-quotas-under-ceta/131825571`): approximately **85% of India's steel exports to the UK are exempt**; the measure touches 15% of India's $839m sectoral exports; **188 items worth $137m** fall under the measure but are given access under the trade pact; India also gets residual (first-come, first-served) quotas and access to the **Authorised Use Scheme**.
> "The resolution of the issue has paved the way for the implementation of the agreement from July 15."

New Indian Express (17 July 2026): India negotiated a dedicated preferential steel quota worth **~$350m**, against historical steel exports of about $200m.

**Pressure track:** India raised the issue at the WTO Council for Trade in Goods on **20–21 May 2026**, alongside Brazil, Turkey, Japan, Korea, Switzerland and Australia. An Indian official described the WTO route as "a parallel discussion" to be settled per the bilateral outcome — and it was. Shashi Mathews (CMS INDUSLAW) had assessed that the UK move "may not technically breach the FTA" and that "the FTA did not carve out steel from such safeguard measures," recommending litigation only as a "last resort." **The resolution was negotiated, not litigated** — which is exactly the template India will have to use on CBAM, since it has even less legal leverage there.

### CBAM stacks on top — confirmed in writing

The implementation notification (`…/implementation-notifications-on-the-transitional-exemption-quota-administration-and-the-ukraine-exclusion`, updated 22 July 2026) is explicit:
> "The UK CBAM is an environmental domestic tax designed to tackle the risk of carbon leakage. It is **separate to** the steel trade measure… The UK CBAM will apply from 1 January 2027 **alongside** the steel trade measure which came into force on 1 July 2026, and **in addition to** current trade remedy measures."

So from 1 Jan 2027 Indian steel faces, cumulatively: the tariff-rate quota, a 50% out-of-quota tariff, CBAM, and any existing anti-dumping/countervailing duties. CETA's zero tariff sits underneath all four.

Ukraine is excluded from the measure. The UK coordinated closely with the EU, whose parallel measure (Regulation (EU) 2026/1384) also took effect 1 July 2026 with an 18.3 Mt quota and 50% over-quota rate.

⚠️ Unverified secondary (indiaeuropa.com, 30 July 2026): Tata Steel UK has complained that the CETA quota concession threatens its Welsh output.
