# TIER 3 — INDIAN LAW & ADVISORY FIRMS WITH UK PRESENCE

## The regulatory reality (drives everything in this tier)

**BCI Rules for Registration and Regulation of Foreign Lawyers and Foreign Law Firms in India, 2022** — notified 10 March 2023; **substantially amended and effectively replaced by gazette notification dated 13 May 2025** after pushback from Indian lawyers, firms and State Bar Councils. Source: LiveLaw / Bar & Bench analysis; BCI gazette at barcouncilofindia.org.

**Permitted:** advisory and transactional work confined to foreign or international law (JVs, M&A, IP, contract drafting); international arbitration representation — but the 2025 Amendment **narrows** this to matters involving foreign law, international law, or a combination (the 2022 Rules allowed it regardless); offices in India on a **reciprocity** basis after registration; partnerships with Indian lawyers only where all parties are registered. Referral arrangements and consultancy contracts do *not* require the Indian side to register.

**Prohibited (registered or not):** advising on Indian law; appearing before Indian courts, tribunals or statutory authorities; conducting litigation; any work requiring BCI/State Bar Council enrolment.

**Fly-in fly-out:** no registration needed, but capped at **60 consecutive calendar days in any 12-month period** (counted from first arrival regardless of exits and re-entries), no office or regular presence, and a mandatory Form C declaration.

**Case law spine:** Madras HC in *A.K. Balaji* (21 Dec 2012) → Supreme Court in *Bar Council of India v. A.K. Balaji* (13 Mar 2018) diluted the blanket permission. The 2022 Rules are under challenge in *Narendra Sharma & Ors. v. Bar Council of India*, WP (C) No. 1556 of 2024 (Delhi HC, not stayed).

**Uptake: lukewarm — only three foreign firms registered in India as of April 2025.**

**Why this matters commercially to you:** The mirror-image constraint is the one that shapes your market. Indian law firms **cannot practise English law**. Any Indian firm marketing "UK entry" is either (a) referring the regulated work to an English firm, or (b) operating a non-SRA-regulated UK entity that can only do unreserved activities. Both leave a wide-open slot for an SRA-regulated (or law-adjacent) UK boutique that can *receive* that referral flow **and** wrap it with corporate services. The reciprocity principle also means the corridor stays intermediated — nobody gets to be a single integrated firm on both sides.

# WHERE LAW FIRMS LEAVE GAPS

1. **Nobody provides a registered office or a director. Zero firms out of ~35.** SRA-regulated firms won't take on directorship because of the personal liability, the conflict with acting as legal adviser to the company, and the AML/beneficial-ownership burden under the ECCTA regime. This is the hardest wall in the market and the single clearest differentiator available to you — an Indian parent that needs a UK-resident director for banking or credibility has literally nowhere in the legal profession to go.

2. **Accounting is offered by exactly one firm (Child & Child) and only as tax-return filing.** Every other firm explicitly punts: Penningtons says it works "with leading Indian law and accountancy firms." The client is therefore forced to run at least two suppliers, and the handoff between the lawyer who forms the entity and the accountant who has to file for it is unmanaged by anyone. That seam is where the client's pain lives.

3. **Not one firm publishes a price. Not one.** Across magic circle, mid-market and two-partner boutiques, the answer is uniformly a "Pricing" link in the footer pointing at SRA transparency boilerplate. Zaiwalla is the only firm that even makes a *relative* price claim ("same quality… at far more competitive rates") and still gives no number. An Indian founder cannot get an indicative UK-entry cost from any law firm website in this market. A published fixed-fee entry package would be genuinely unique.

4. **The economics price out the actual inbound flow.** The firms with real capability serve Reliance, Tata, Aditya Birla, Hinduja and £92.5m property deals. Penningtons — the best mid-market inbound practice — uses no SME or startup language anywhere. The Indian mid-cap doing a £2–15m UK subsidiary or bolt-on has capable advisers above it and unnamed one-man India desks below it, with nothing calibrated in between.

5. **Incorporation is treated as beneath the profession, never as a product.** Firms write "establishment of subsidiaries, businesses, group structures" (Penningtons) or "setting up a UK branch for an overseas company" (Child & Child) — advisory framings of a mechanical task. No firm sells company formation as a deliverable with a price and a turnaround time, because it isn't billable enough to justify partner attention. It is, however, the first thing the client actually needs.

6. **Business immigration is the most commonly bundled adjacent service and the most unevenly delivered.** Child & Child and Greenwich name specific routes (Expansion Worker, Sponsor Licence, GBM, Innovator Founder); Shakespeare Martineau and Trowers offer only the vague label "global mobility"; Albertson, gunnercooke and Zaiwalla don't do it at all. Since UK Expansion Worker is *the* route an Indian parent uses to seed a UK subsidiary, and it requires a UK footprint the client doesn't have yet, immigration and incorporation are the same problem — and almost nobody sells them as one.

7. **India desks are being quietly wound down, and named ownership is thin.** CMS's India desk page is HTTP 410 Gone. Ashurst and HSF Kramer have no India landing page at all. Mishcon's experience examples are years stale. LKS closed its London office in 2019. Sannam S4 sold its corporate-services arm to TMF. Meanwhile Greenwich and Lawsmith name **no lawyers whatsoever**, and Child & Child — the strongest boutique — fronts its desk with a "Client Lead," not a partner. The corridor is under-served precisely because it is being deprioritised.

8. **The regulatory split guarantees a permanent intermediary role, and nobody is filling it well.** BCI rules mean Indian firms cannot practise English law and are capped at 60 fly-in days a year; SRA firms can't sit on Indian client boards or file Indian compliance. Every large firm openly says it "works alongside Indian firms." So the corridor is *necessarily* a two-firm affair — and the referral-receiving slot is currently occupied either by City firms too expensive for the mid-market, or by unregulated Indian-branded UK entities like Singhania UK (explicitly "not regulated by the SRA") and Lex Meridian (no regulatory disclosure at all) that are chasing exactly your client size while being unable to do the regulated work. **A regulated, priced, bundled UK boutique sits in an empty room.**
