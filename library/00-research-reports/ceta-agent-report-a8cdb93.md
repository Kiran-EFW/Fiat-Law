## 1. THE DOUBLE CONTRIBUTIONS CONVENTION

### 1.1 It is 60 months, not 36. This is the single most-misreported fact in the deal.

The chronology, resolved definitively from primary sources:

| Date | Instrument | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| 6 May 2025 | UK conclusion summary | "up to 3 years" |
| 10 Feb 2026 | **Treaty as signed**, Art.8(1): *"provided that the anticipated duration of such work does not exceed 36 months"* (CS India No.2/2026, CP 1513) | 36 months |
| **6 July 2026** | **Exchange of Notes**, British High Commission New Delhi, NOTE NO: CH/551/26: *"Article 8 of the Agreement shall be amended as follows: '36 months' wherever it appears shall be substituted by '60 months'. An equivalent amendment shall also be made to the Hindi version of the Agreement."* | **60 months** |
| 15 July 2026 | Agreement + Exchange of Notes enter into force (Treaty Series No.31 (2026), CP 1655) | **60 months** |

UK domestic effect: SI 2026/274 (NI equivalent SI 2026/50) gave the treaty effect; **SI 2026/794 (NI equivalent 2026/148) raised the detached-worker period from 36 to 60 months**. Source: HMRC NIM33251, https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/national-insurance-manual/nim33251

Any adviser, blog or 2025-vintage article still saying "three years" is out of date. 36 months now survives only in Art.12 (case-by-case exceptions agreed between the competent authorities).

### 1.2 Who qualifies (Article 8)

- Employee of an employer in one country, **sent by that employer** to work in the other, remaining employed and paid by the home-country employer, where the **anticipated duration does not exceed 60 months**. Continue paying home-country contributions only; exempt in the host.
- **Prior-contribution condition:** must have been subject to home-country legislation and paying contributions **for at least 30 days before departure**, or for the preceding calendar month (HMRC NIM33258, https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/national-insurance-manual/nim33258).
- **Art.8(4) is generous:** it expressly covers persons sent from a **third country** (e.g. an Indian national already on assignment in Singapore for the Indian employer, then moved to the UK) and persons who go abroad *"for reasons of personal choice … with the agreement of their employer."*
- **Art.8(5) cooling-off:** six months must elapse between detachments; a shorter prior detachment permits a proportionately shorter gap.
- **Art.8(6)/(7):** contributions are payable on **total remuneration / total wages** — for India, "wages" as defined in **s.2(88) of the Code on Social Security 2020**, not Indian base salary.
- **Excluded:** the **self-employed** ("The agreement does not apply to self-employment" — NIM33251). Also excluded: Indian nationals **hired directly by a UK employer** (no home-country employer, no detachment). Family members who take UK employment pay UK NICs.
- Mariners (Art.9, flag state, with reversal where the worker resides in the other country and is paid there), aircrew (Art.10, home base), government employees/armed forces (Art.11, sending state).
- Voluntary contributions in the other state are barred for the same period (Art.13); CF83 remains possible for people *living but not working* in India or living-and-self-employed in India.
- Duration: indefinite (Art.27). Certificates: **HMRC form CA9107** for UK-legislation workers; **EPFO** issues the Certificate of Coverage for India-legislation workers (portal: epfoiwu.epfindia.gov.in/iwu). The UK is now listed among EPFO's operating SSA countries.

Sources: https://www.gov.uk/guidance/uk-and-india-new-social-security-agreement (HMRC, 7 July 2026); DCC explainer https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/uk-india-trade-deal-double-contributions-convention-explainer/uk-india-double-contributions-convention-dcc-explainer

### 1.3 CRITICAL TRANSITIONAL TRAP

**Anyone already on assignment in the UK on 15 July 2026 is NOT a detached worker.** They fall under Art.7(2) (law of the place of work) and become liable for UK NICs from 15 July 2026 — *even if they were mid-way through the domestic 52-week exemption*. Mirror-image for UK employees in India (Class 1 liability ceases; they move to Indian legislation).

- HMRC NIM33252: https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/national-insurance-manual/nim33252
- KPMG GMS Flash Alert 2026-154: *"The DCC will not apply to individuals who are already on assignment on the day the agreement enters into force."*

If you have people in the UK today who arrived before 15 July 2026, they got **no** benefit and, if they had exemption time left, they got worse off. The fix for such a person is generally to end the assignment, observe the six-month gap, and re-detach.

### 1.4 Quantifying the saving — do the arithmetic properly

The headline framing is inflated. PIB says Indian professionals and employers *"contributed nearly 23% of salaries to the UK's National Insurance system."* That is a headline-rate figure (8% + 15%) applied as if to the whole salary; it ignores the primary threshold (£12,570), the secondary threshold (£5,000) and the 2% band above the UEL (£50,270).

**Actual UK NIC cost, 2026/27 rates** (employee 8% between £12,570 and £50,270 then 2%; employer 15% above £5,000 — https://www.gov.uk/guidance/rates-and-thresholds-for-employers-2026-to-2027):

| Gross salary | Employer NIC | Employee NIC | **Total per year** | Effective rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| £52,500 (current SNR minimum) | £7,125 | £3,061 | **£10,186** | 19.4% |
| £75,000 | £10,500 | £3,511 | **£14,011** | 18.7% |
| £100,000 | £14,250 | £4,011 | **£18,261** | 18.3% |

**But the marginal benefit is smaller than the gross figure**, because the UK already granted a **52-week** exemption unilaterally (regs 145(2)/146, Social Security (Contributions) Regulations 2001) for workers not ordinarily resident and seconded by a non-UK employer. So the DCC buys **months 13–60 — four additional years:**

| Gross salary | 4 extra years of NIC exemption |
|---|---|
| £52,500 | **≈ £40,700** |
| £75,000 | **≈ £56,000** |
| £100,000 | **≈ £73,000** |

A three-year assignment at £52,500 saves roughly £30,600 (≈ ₹34 lakh) — which cross-checks Business Today's widely-cited *"Rs 30-40 lakh per individual"* (https://www.businesstoday.in/india/story/india-uk-fta-double-contribution-convention-explained-483676-2025-07-25). Note that figure was calculated against the 36-month version and understates the 60-month position.

**Aggregate claims (all soft, none officially costed):**
- PIB: benefits *"over 75,000 Indian professionals and around 900 Indian companies"*; *"Industry estimates suggest current annual saving of more than USD 600 million."*
- Business Standard headline: *"£500 million annual savings."* India Briefing: ~US$500m/yr.
- **The UK impact assessment does NOT quantify the DCC** — no Exchequer cost, no Mode 4 figure. https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/uk-india-free-trade-agreement-impact-assessment/uk-india-free-trade-agreement-impact-assessment-executive-summary

⚠️ **Flag:** these are industry estimates, not government figures, and they diverge by a factor implied only by different assumptions on headcount and salary. Do not treat US$600m as verified.

### 1.5 The offsetting cost nobody advertises

Art.8(7) requires the detached worker to keep contributing in India **on total wages**, not on the ₹15,000 EPF ceiling. Combined EPF is ~24% (12% employee + 12% employer). On an India-payrolled worker whose UK-assignment package is loaded, **the Indian contribution can exceed the UK NIC saved in pure cash-flow terms** — though EPF is the employee's own accumulating savings, whereas NICs are a pure tax from which detached workers build **no UK State Pension entitlement** (gov.uk explainer confirms this expressly).

The gov.uk explainer also states flatly that the DCC *"will not make it cheaper to hire Indian workers over British workers"* — because IHS (£1,035/yr), the Immigration Skills Charge (£1,000 first 12 months + £500 per further 6 months), the CoS fee (£525) and visa fees are untouched.

⚠️ **NEW FINDING — the Indian side of this is under Supreme Court challenge and genuinely unsettled:**
- **Karnataka HC, 25 April 2024**, *Stone Hill Education Foundation v Union of India*, W.P. 18486/2012: struck down **Para 83 EPF Scheme 1952** and **Para 43A EPS 1995** (the "international worker" no-ceiling regime) as violating Art.14. Binds only in Karnataka / for the petitioners.
- **Delhi HC, 4 November 2025**, *SpiceJet Ltd v UoI* (W.P. 2941/2012) and *LG Electronics India v UoI*: Division Bench **upheld** the same notifications. (Cyril Amarchand client alert: https://www.cyrilshroff.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Client-Alert-Del-HC-Judgement-on-IW-Regime.pdf; EY alert https://www.ey.com/en_in/technical/alerts-hub/2025/11/delhi-high-court-ruling-on-international-worker-provisions-under-the-provident-fund-law)
- **Supreme Court took up the conflict on 12 March 2026** and issued notice to the Union; unresolved as at 4 August 2026. EPFO continues to enforce full-salary contributions everywhere outside the specific orders. ⚠️ *The 12 March 2026 SC date comes from a single secondary source (nrifinancialservices.com) — the HC split itself is firmly sourced, the SC listing date is not. Verify before relying.*

This matters because the DCC's Art.8(7) obligation to pay on total wages is an obligation to pay under *Indian* law as it stands — and Indian law on exactly that point is before the Supreme Court.

## 2. MODE 4 / MOVEMENT OF NATURAL PERSONS

Chapter 10 + Annex 10A. Structural provisions first:

- **Art.10.3(3):** the chapter *"shall not apply to measures affecting natural persons seeking access to the employment market of a Party nor … citizenship, nationality, residence, or employment on a permanent basis."*
- **Art.10.4(3) — the ENT/quota prohibition:** *"unless otherwise specified in Annex 10A … neither Party shall adopt or maintain limitations on the total number of natural persons of the other Party to be granted temporary entry, in the form of numerical quotas or the requirement of an economic needs test."*
- **Art.10.4(5):** entry does not exempt anyone from professional licensing requirements.
- **Art.10.9:** dispute settlement only where there is *"a pattern of practice"* AND administrative remedies are exhausted. A single refusal is not actionable.
- Art.10.8: Working Group meets within one year of EIF (i.e. by 15 July 2027).

### 2.1 UK schedule (Annex 10A(ii)) — what India got

| Category | Duration | Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| **Business visitors for establishment purposes** | 90 days in any 6-month period | No work permit; **cannot receive UK remuneration** |
| **Short-term business visitors** | 90 days in any 6-month period | 4 permitted activity types: meetings/consultations; sales; trade fairs/exhibitions; after-sales/after-lease service |
| **Intra-corporate transferees — managers & specialists** | *"temporary stay for a period not exceeding three years, with possible extension at the discretion of the United Kingdom"* | ≥1 year prior employment with the group. **Partner may work, employed or self-employed, without a work permit; dependent children admitted for the same period** |
| **ICTs — graduate trainees** | 1 year | ≥6 months prior employment |
| **Investors** | 1 year | Investor needs 12 months' prior employment; the Indian juridical person must have traded ≥3 years. *"Grant of temporary entry is based on connection to the economy rather than the amount of capital."* |
| **Contractual service suppliers (CSS)** | *"cumulative period of not more than 12 months in any 24-month period or for the duration of the contract, whichever is less"* | University degree + **3 years'** professional experience. **33 sub-sectors** |
| **Independent professionals (IP)** | 12 months in any 24-month period | **6 years'** professional experience. **16 sub-sectors** |

### 2.2 The ONLY numerical cap in the entire agreement

> *"Temporary entry shall be granted for up to a combined total of **1,800 per year** of qualified, professional Indian traditional chefs, classical musicians, and yoga instructors."*

This is now hard-coded into the Immigration Rules (see §3.2). Everything else is uncapped and ENT-free, as PIB confirms: *"The UK has agreed not to impose numerical restrictions or an Economic Needs Test (ENT)."*

⚠️ Note: the 1,800 is **not** a general Indian-professional quota, contrary to a good deal of press. It is a niche cultural-occupations allocation.

### 2.3 India's schedule (Annex 10A(i)) — what the UK got

- Business visitors: **up to 180 days**, extendable at India's discretion.
- **ICTs: 1 year or contract length, extendable year-to-year up to a maximum of 5 years** — materially better than the UK's 3-year binding.
- CSS: 1 year or contract duration. **27 sub-sectors.**
- Independent Professionals: 1 year (12 months + max 3-month extension). **12 sub-sectors.**
- Installer and Servicer: 3 months.
- Spouses/dependants of ICTs admitted for an equal period where the stay is ≥12 months; **working rights subject to domestic law** (i.e. not guaranteed — weaker than the UK's unconditional partner work right).
- **Both India's CSS and IP lists exclude legal services**, and management consulting is scheduled *"excluding all services relating to legal consultancy."*

### 2.4 Coverage claims

PIB: India secured UK commitments across *"all 12 major service sectors and 137 sub-sectors … over 99 per cent of India's export interests"*; India extended commitments in *"108 sub-sectors."*

## 3. VISA ROUTES — precisely what changed and what did not

### 3.1 No new visa route. Full stop.

The decisive source is the UK government's own Business Mobility explainer (updated 15 July 2026):

> **"We have not created any new visa routes in this agreement."**
> **"Nothing in this deal affects the UK's point-based immigration system."**

https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/uk-india-trade-deal-business-mobility-explainer

Mapping of CETA categories onto **pre-existing** routes:

| CETA category | UK route used | Reality check |
|---|---|---|
| Business visitors | **Standard Visitor** (6 months) | UK's applied regime is *more* generous than the 90-day binding |
| ICT managers/specialists | **GBM Senior or Specialist Worker** | CETA guarantees ≥3 years; UK's applied rules already allow 5 yrs in 6 (9 in 10 for high earners). The explainer concedes the binding is *"lower than what is currently available under the UK system"* |
| Graduate trainees | **GBM Graduate Trainee** (12 months) | Unchanged |
| Investors | **GBM UK Expansion Worker** (1 yr, max 2) | Unchanged |
| CSS / Independent professionals | **GBM Service Supplier** | **This is the only route that actually changed** |

The explainer further states: *"The deal does not lock in any sponsorship processes or criteria"*; salary thresholds and surcharges are not bound; *"we have not made any changes to the UK's surcharges"*; *"we will not be creating or ringfencing jobs for Indian professionals."* 25,117 GBM visas were granted worldwide in 2024. *"Nothing in the deal is expected to have a long-term impact on net migration."*

### 3.2 The actual Immigration Rules changes: HC 1691 (5 March 2026), APP GBM1–GBM5

All five are confined to the **Service Supplier** route. Verbatim:

- **APP GBM1** — after SSU 3.2 insert: *"SSU 3.3. The number of granted applications (for both entry clearance and permission to stay) to Indian nationals for the job roles traditional chef, classical musicians or a yoga instructor, must not have exceeded 1,800 in total, the annual total allocation of places for the route (where the annual period begins on 1 January and ends on 31 December of each year)."*
- **APP GBM2/3** — after SSU 8.1(g) insert: *"(ga) where the service that the applicant will provide is covered by a commitment in the UK-India Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement (CETA), a national of India; or"* — i.e. Indian nationals become an eligible nationality on the route.
- **APP GBM4** — after SSU 13.2(c)(iib) insert: *"(iic) if the applicant is an Indian national covered by a relevant commitment in the UK-India Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement (CETA), **12 months**; or"* — versus the 6-month default that applies to most other trade agreements.
- **APP GBM5** — corresponding extension-of-stay computation.
- **In force 26 March 2026** (applications made before that date decided under the rules in force on 25 March 2026).

Separately in HC 1691: **APP GBM6–8** change Secondment Worker SEC 7.2(b)/7.3/7.3(a) from "12 months" to "6 months", effective 8 April 2026 — **not** CETA-related.

Also in HC 1691, and frequently confused with CETA: **Appendix Youth Mobility Scheme 2026 allocations — "India – 3,000 places."** This derives from the **2021 Migration and Mobility Partnership**, not CETA.

HC 259 (9 July 2026) contains one India item only: `APP V3. After V 15A.4(e), insert: "(f) a national of India who holds a diplomatic passport issued by India."` (visa exemption for Indian diplomatic passport holders). Nothing else.

**Nothing changed for Senior or Specialist Worker, Graduate Trainee, UK Expansion Worker or Skilled Worker in respect of India.**

### 3.3 What India conspicuously did NOT get

- **No waiver of the 12-month prior-overseas-employment requirement on UK Expansion Worker.** Japan (CEPA) and Australia (FTA) nationals have that waiver written into Appendix GBM. **India does not.** This is a real operational cost for a first-time UK entrant.
- No reduction in salary thresholds, IHS, ISC or visa fees.
- No settlement route. No new dependant rights beyond what GBM already gives.
- India remains a **visa national** (Appendix Visitor: Visa National List, entry 47, updated 3 August 2026) — so "90 days visa-free business visits" is wrong; a Standard Visitor visa is still required.

### 3.4 Live thresholds (Appendix Global Business Mobility, as at August 2026)

| Route | Salary | Max stay | Prior service |
|---|---|---|---|
| Senior or Specialist Worker | **£52,500** + 100% of going rate | 5 yrs in any 6; **9 yrs in any 10 if earning ≥£73,900** | 12 cumulative months overseas in the group (waived for high earners) |
| Graduate Trainee | **£27,300** | 1 year | 3 months continuous |
| UK Expansion Worker | **£52,500** | 1 year, renewable to 2 max | 12 months (no India waiver) |
| Service Supplier | 40 points (20 sponsorship + 20 skill) | **12 months for CETA-covered Indian nationals** | — |

⚠️ **The gov.uk explainers are stale.** Both the Business Mobility explainer and the DCC explainer quote **£48,500** and **£25,410**. The live Immigration Rules say **£52,500** and **£27,300**. Use the Rules.

⚠️ **Unresolved contradiction in the gov.uk explainer.** It says *"No UK mobility visa routes offer a path to settlement and this deal does not change that"* and then, in the next breath, that GBM time *"will contribute towards meeting the residence requirement for settlement in the UK."* The Immigration Rules and the SNR visa overview page both say the route is **not** a route to settlement and you cannot apply for ILR on it. Most likely reconciliation: GBM time can count toward the separate **10-year long residence** route. Treat the second sentence as an imprecisely-worded reference to that, not as a settlement pathway.

## 4. SERVICES SECTORS

### 4.1 The framework

- Chapter 8 (Trade in Services) + Annex 8B (schedules of specific commitments), Annex 8C (MFN), **Annex 8A (Professional Services)**.
- **Annex 8A is best-endeavours only.** Art.8A.4(1): each Party shall within **12 months** of EIF identify professions of mutual interest and encourage the relevant bodies to negotiate MRAs *"with a view to concluding those negotiations within 36 months."* Art.8A.5: temporary/project-specific licensing — *"may consider, if feasible."* Working Group meets 12 months after EIF. **There is no automatic recognition of any qualification.**
- PIB names the MRA targets: **nursing, accountancy, architecture**. The UK conclusion summary's PBS annex names *"accountants, auditors, architects, solicitors and barristers, engineers"* — but explicitly with **no automatic mutual recognition**.

### 4.2 LEGAL SERVICES — the deal's biggest asymmetry

**India bound nothing.** Verified directly from `/tmp/an8b_india.txt`:
- India's Annex 8B contains **no legal services entry at all**.
- Management consulting (CPC 865) is scheduled *"excluding all services relating to legal consultancy."*
- India's **Annex 8C MFN list omits legal services entirely** — so if India later opens its legal market to a third country, **the UK gets no automatic pull-through**. (The UK's MFN list, by contrast, *does* include "(a) Legal services (CPC 861)".)
- India's Mode 4 CSS and IP lists both exclude legal services.

**The UK bound everything.** From `/tmp/an8b_uk.txt`:
- *"a) 1. Home country law, other foreign law and international law (part of CPC 861)"* — Modes 1, 2, 3: **None** (i.e. fully open).
- *"a) 2. Domestic UK law (excluding … notaries)"* — Mode 1 None except that commercial presence or residency may be required by the relevant professional body for **reserved activities** and **immigration advice**; Mode 3 None except non-discriminatory legal form requirements.
- Footnote 3 defines legal services broadly: advisory, representation, arbitration/conciliation/mediation, documentation and certification.
- **Indian lawyers get UK Mode 4 access:** "legal advisory services in respect of home country law, other foreign law and public international law (part of CPC 861)" is item (a) on the UK's **Contractual Service Supplier** list AND on the **Independent Professional** list, and India is marked "Yes" against **legal advisory services** in both Table A and Table B of the operational gov.uk guidance: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/global-business-mobility-service-supplier-relevant-trade-agreements-and-sectors (the guidance adds a new code **IND = UK-India CETA**).

So: **an Indian law firm can now send Indian-qualified lawyers to the UK for 12 months in any 24 to advise on Indian law, but a UK firm still cannot open in India.**

**Reaction.** Bar Council of England & Wales, 7 May 2025, Barbara Mills KC: *"we are really disappointed that it does not extend to legal services … This was a once-in-a-lifetime opportunity."* The Bar Council has an MoU with the Bar Council of India signed 6 June 2023.

**The Indian regulatory position (which is where the real action is, not CETA):**
1. **BCI Rules for Registration and Regulation of Foreign Lawyers and Foreign Law Firms in India, 2022** — gazetted **10 March 2023**.
2. **Draft Advocates (Amendment) Bill, 2025** — would have shifted control from the BCI to central government; **withdrawn after advocate protests**.
3. **Gazette notification 13 May 2025, F. No. BCI:D:3335/2025** — amended and effectively replaced the 2022 Rules. Key changes (per Majmudar & Partners, https://www.majmudarindia.com/wp-content/uploads/2025-05-23-BCI-Rules-Entry-of-Foreign-Law-Firms-in-India.pdf):
   - Creates a new **"Indian-foreign law firm"** category — an Indian entity that, on registration, may practise both Indian law and non-litigious foreign law.
   - Allows **Indian advocates to register as "foreign lawyers"** without losing their right of audience in Indian courts.
   - Expands the permitted non-litigious scope (JVs, M&A, IP, contract drafting) with detailed permitted/prohibited lists.
   - **Tightens fly-in fly-out**: still 60 days aggregate in any 12 months, but now must not constitute "regular practice" and requires a mandatory **Form C declaration to the BCI for every visit**. Reuters headlined this as *"India issues stricter rules for foreign lawyers flying in for client work"* (15 May 2025).
   - Formal partnerships between foreign and Indian firms require the Indian side to register; unregistered Indian firms may only use **referral arrangements or engagement contracts**.
   - 5-year registration; **renewal may be refused/limited to preserve reciprocity balance**.
4. **BCI press release 18 June 2025** defending the rules: *"these Rules do not allow foreign lawyers to practise Indian law."*
5. **October 2025** — BCI issued a fresh public warning against **unregistered collaborations** between Indian and foreign law firms (SCC Online, 23 Oct 2025; Law.com, 22 Oct 2025).
6. **Vires challenge pending:** *Narendra Sharma & Ors* writ in the Delhi High Court challenging whether the BCI has power under the Advocates Act 1961 to authorise foreign lawyers in non-litigious matters. Notices issued to BCI and the Ministry of Law and Justice; adjourned to 14 August 2025. ⚠️ **Post-August-2025 status not verified.** If the BCI is held to lack vires, the whole 2022/2025 framework falls.
7. **21 July 2026 — Draft Advocates (Amendment) Bill, 2026**, released by the BCI for public consultation. This is a **retreat**:
   - Moves the foreign-lawyer framework out of BCI rules and **into the Advocates Act itself**, with BCI rules on registration and fly-in/fly-out requiring **prior Central Government approval**.
   - Expressly bars foreign lawyers and firms from appearing before Indian courts, tribunals, authorities, from practising Indian law and from litigation. **Sole exception: appearance before an arbitral tribunal in an international commercial arbitration where foreign law governs the substance.**
   - Bans foreigners from enrolling as Indian advocates; registration ≠ enrolment on a State roll.
   - Retains **reciprocity** as a bar: citizens/firms of a country that discriminates against Indian lawyers may be excluded.
   - Introduces mandatory statutory **registration of all Indian law firms** with the BCI/State Bar Councils.
   - BCI: *"The doors of India would only be opened up for foreign law firms in a phased manner — ie in a way which could suit the Indian law firms."*
   - Sources: https://www.barandbench.com/news/law-firms-in-india-must-register-with-bar-councils-bci-proposes-changes-to-advocates-act ; Law Society Gazette, 24 July 2026, *"Fresh blow to India legal market liberalisation hopes"*, https://www.lawgazette.co.uk/news/fresh-blow-to-india-legal-market-liberalisation-hopes/5127490.article

The Gazette also reports that **earlier in 2026 the UK government said legal services would form part of future trade talks with India** — i.e. this is parked for a future round, not settled.

**UK-side position for Indian lawyers (background, not CETA-created):** advising on foreign/international law in England & Wales is an unreserved activity requiring no local qualification. Registered Foreign Lawyer status (s.89 Courts and Legal Services Act 1990) permits partnership in SRA-regulated firms. Indian-qualified lawyers can qualify as solicitors via the SQE. ⚠️ *This paragraph is established background I did not re-verify against a primary source in this session.*

### 4.3 Other sectors — India's schedule (Annex 8B)

| Sector | India's binding |

| **Telecoms** | Mode 1 Unbound. Mode 3: incorporation in India + licence; *"maximum foreign investment up to 100 percent. For foreign investment more than 49%, Government approval would be required."* CTO/CSO/lawful-interception officers must be Indian citizens; majority of directors Indian; security vetting of Chairman/MD/CEO/CFO; min. two licences per service area; no resale of telephony; unbound for PSUs. **This is the one sector bound at 100%** |
| **Higher education, CPC 923** (privately funded only) | Mode 3: None, subject to fees being fixable by an appropriate authority, no capitation fees or profiteering, and state/local regulation |
| **Single Brand Product Retail** | Mode 3 bound at only **51%** — well *below* India's applied 100% automatic route |
| **Horizontal** | MA/NT restrictions in the **Consolidated FDI Policy Circular 2020 (latest)** and **FEMA** regulations *"as revised and amended from time to time"* continue to apply, provided changes are not applied *"so as to nullify or impair the benefits."* At least one director and one manager must be resident in India |

**Financial services (Chapter 9 / Annex 9A):**
- **Insurance** — life, non-life, reinsurance, Mode 3: *"commercial presence would be through incorporation of a company with foreign investment not exceeding 74 per cent."* Above 49%: 50% of net profit to general reserve if solvency <1.2× control level; ≥50% independent directors. NT: majority of directors, KMPs and one of Chairperson/MD/CEO must be **Resident Indian Citizens**.
- **Banking** — **"Unbound for branches of foreign banks"** for both market access and national treatment. Legal form limited to a wholly-owned subsidiary (subject to ENTs, reciprocity, inclusive-banking requirements) or FDI in a private bank capped at **74%** (49% automatic, above that Government/RBI approval). WOS may hold ≤5% of an Indian private bank; new WOS entry restrictable where foreign banks exceed 20% of system capital. **UK banks got no new branch access.**
- **Asset management** — cash/portfolio management, collective investment, custodial, depository and trust services (**excluding pension fund management**): Mode 3 requires incorporation in India, but **national treatment Mode 3 is "None"**. Cleanest financial commitment in the schedule.
- **Transferable securities** — JV with foreign investment ≤74%, limited to recognised foreign stock-broking companies.
- **Payment systems, Mode 1** — *"Access to respective Fast Payment Systems (FPS) on a reciprocal basis, without a need to get onboarded onto the other payment system, through interlinking of FPS for: (i) QR code-based merchant payments, and (ii) instant fund transfers"* (UPI ↔ Faster Payments).
- **Chapter 9 contains NO MFN article and no ratchet.**

⚠️ **India's applied law has already overtaken its binding.** The Sabka Bima Sabki Raksha (Amendment of Insurance Laws) Act 2025 was gazetted 3 February 2026, and **FEMA (Non-debt Instruments) (Second Amendment) Rules, 2026, S.O. 2186(E), dated 2 May 2026, permit 100% FDI in insurance companies and intermediaries under the automatic route** (LIC still capped at 20%), subject to a resident chair-or-CEO condition and IRDAI verification. Aviva became the first foreign insurer to fully own its Indian life JV around 9 July 2026. **Because Chapter 9 has no MFN and no standstill/ratchet, CETA does not lock the 100% regime in.** India could revert to 74% without breaching CETA.

**Digital trade (Ch.12):** source code protection secured; **no binding data-flow or data-localisation rules** — only a future-review hook. This is a significant gap for Indian IT services firms and UK tech.

**Services standstill:** the UK conclusion summary confirms a services standstill (no backsliding from applied levels) but with the FDI-policy carve-out above cutting across it on India's side.

### 4.4 Trade context

Bilateral services trade US$35.44bn in 2024 — India exports US$21.66bn, imports US$13.78bn, **surplus US$7.88bn** (PIB).

## 5. GOVERNMENT PROCUREMENT (Chapter 15 + Annex 15A)

### 5.1 Genuinely a first

India is **not** a WTO GPA party (observer only). CETA is **India's first FTA with a binding procurement market-access schedule**. business.gov.uk: *"India's first trade deal with a comprehensive government procurement chapter and market access schedule."* Covered Indian federal entities publish *"on average, approximately 40,000 tenders per year with a value of at least £38 billion"*; India spends ~20% of GDP on public procurement. PIB frames it as UK £90bn / US$122bn access for Indian suppliers versus US$114bn Indian access for the UK.

### 5.2 Thresholds

| | Goods | Services | Construction |
|---|---|---|---|
| **India** (Section A, central govt) | **SDR 450,000** | **SDR 450,000** | **SDR 5,000,000** |
| **UK** (Section A) | **SDR 130,000** | **SDR 130,000** | **SDR 5,000,000** |
| **UK** (Section C, airports/ports) | SDR 400,000 | SDR 400,000 | SDR 5,000,000 |

Indicative equivalents (business.gov.uk, not treaty text): India SDR 450,000 ≈ ₹5.5 crore / £480,000; SDR 5m ≈ ₹62 crore / £5.3m; UK SDR 130,000 ≈ £138,000.

**UK raised-bar services**: hotel/lodging (CPC 641), food & beverage (642/643), **legal (861)**, government administration (91136, 9127), education (92), recreational/cultural/sporting (96) are covered only above **£663,540** (Section A) / **£884,720** (Section C).

**Floor:** Art.15.22(6) — neither Party can ever be required below SDR 130,000/130,000/5,000,000 for central entities. India's SDR 450,000 sits above the floor and is a live target for future reduction under the Art.15.22 further-negotiations/ratchet clause.

⚠️ **The "Rs 200 crore" confusion.** Piyush Goyal said in May 2025 that UK firms could bid *"only where orders exceed Rs 200 crore."* That figure is the **pre-existing domestic Global Tender Enquiry bar under GFR 2017 Rule 161(iv)**, as amended by Dept of Expenditure OM F.12/17/2019-PPD of 15 May 2020 ("no GTE shall be invited for tenders up to Rs 200 crore"). It was a conclusion-stage statement, superseded by the ratified Annex. **No Department of Expenditure circular carving CETA procurement out of Rule 161(iv) could be located.** Until one exists there is a live operational question whether Indian entities will actually run global tenders at ₹5.5 crore.

⚠️ India's own INR threshold notification under Annex 15A.i Section H (due before EIF, re-notified at least every two years using IMF rates) could not be located.

### 5.3 India's coverage

- **Section A: 51 numbered central entities** (47 ministries + Dept of Administrative Reforms & Public Grievances, Dept of Pension & Pensioners' Welfare, Dept of Official Language, **Dept of Atomic Energy**), plus ~37 named sub-departments. **Ministry of Railways (35) and Ministry of Road Transport & Highways (36) are in** — the largest procurement lines.
- **Excluded by omission:** Ministry of Defence, Ministry of Home Affairs, **MeitY**, Department of Space, Ministry of Mines, PMO/Cabinet Secretariat.
- **CPSEs — counterintuitive.** All CPSEs of listed entities are covered *"except any CPSE exposed to competitive market forces in any of its operations"* (three-factor test: competitors present; market-based pricing; no legal barrier to entry). **So the commercially attractive CPSEs — ONGC, IOCL, NTPC, BHEL, SAIL, Coal India — are effectively OUT.** The 28 indicatively-covered (non-exposed) CPSEs are the monopolistic/statutory ones: Food Corporation of India, Konkan Railway, Rail Vikas Nigam, DFCCIL, IRFC, Mumbai Railway Vikas, NHIDCL, BIRAC, Uranium Corporation of India, various F&DCs, NEDFi, CERSAI, etc. **Security Printing and Minting Corporation of India, its subsidiaries and Bank Note Paper Mill India are expressly excluded.**
- **Section B (Sub-Central Government Entities): "Nil." Section C (All Other Entities): "Nil."** No states, no municipalities, no state PSUs.

### 5.4 Make in India — Class-II status (Art.15.4(8))

Art.15.4(7) bans offsets. Art.15.4(8) carves India back in:

> *"Notwithstanding paragraphs 1, 2, and 7, India and its procuring entities shall be entitled to seek, take account of, impose, or enforce the MII Order at any stage of a covered procurement, provided that India and its procuring entities treat any supplier of the United Kingdom as a 'Class-II local supplier' under the MII Order where: (a) that supplier offers goods or services having a country of origin as the United Kingdom; and (b) the goods or services have equal to or greater than the minimum 'local content' prescribed for Class-II local suppliers in the MII Order where 'local content' means the amount of value added in the United Kingdom …"*

- MII Order = Public Procurement (Preference to Make in India) Order 2017, No. P-45021/2/2017-PP (BE-II), as revised 16 September 2020. **Class-I = ≥50% local content; Class-II = ≥20%.**
- **Footnote 3: UK value-add can be combined with Indian value-add** — *"this provision shall not prevent a supplier of the United Kingdom from contributing towards the minimum local content in India."*
- Effect: a UK supplier with ≥20% UK content escapes the "non-local supplier" tier but **does not reach Class-I**, which retains first call. business.gov.uk calls this *"a first for any trade partner."*
- Art.15.4(9): India may amend the MII Order provided UK treatment stays *"at least equivalent."*
- **Reciprocal:** Art.15.4(10)–(11) preserve the UK's **Social Value** regime, provided Indian suppliers are treated no less favourably, criteria are objectively verifiable, and self-certification routes are equally open.

### 5.5 India's MSME 25% set-aside is fully preserved

Annex 15A.i Section G(1)(b): Chapter 15 does not apply to *"preferences provided under the Public Procurement Policy for Micro and Small Enterprises (MSEs) Order, 2012, **as amended from time to time**."* Open-ended — India can expand it. That Order reserves 25% of central procurement for MSEs (incl. 4% SC/ST, 3% women), 358 items exclusively, plus a 15% price-preference band. UK suppliers compete only for the non-reserved portion.

**UK tit-for-tat:** Annex 15A.ii Section G Note 4 — Art.15.19 (Domestic Review Procedures) does not apply to Indian suppliers contesting awards to UK SMEs *"until such time as the United Kingdom accepts that India no longer operates discriminatory measures in favour of certain domestic small and minority businesses."*

### 5.6 UK coverage for Indian firms

- **48 Section A central bodies** including **DHSC with NHS England, NHS Trusts and NHS Foundation Trusts**, DfT with National Highways, DESNZ with the Nuclear Decommissioning Authority, HMRC, Royal Mint, plus Scottish, Welsh and Northern Ireland Ministers. **UK Space Agency procurement not covered.**
- **Section B: "Nil."** No English local authorities.
- **Section C** is narrow: airport/terminal facilities for air carriers and maritime/inland port facilities only. **Not** water, energy, urban transport, rail or postal.
- **Section E services: a positive list of 25 CPC categories.** Note 2 excludes core legal representation/advice, notarial and court-designated legal services. Note 3 excludes **human health services (CPC 931)**, administrative healthcare (91122) and nursing/medical personnel supply (87206, 87209) — **clinical NHS services are out; NHS goods and non-clinical services are in.** Note 4 excludes exclusive-right services, fiscal agency/public-debt and arbitration/conciliation.
- **Versus the UK's GPA schedule this is narrower** (no Annex 2 sub-central, only a slice of the utilities annex) — **but since India is not a GPA party, 100% of it is new access for Indian suppliers.** *(GPA comparison is reasoned inference from reading both schedules; no UK government document states it in those terms.)*
- **UK implementation:** the Explanatory Memorandum states the UK specifies the Treaty under **s.89 of the Procurement Act 2023**, making Indian businesses **"treaty state suppliers."** Portal: Find a Tender (www.find-tender.service.gov.uk).

### 5.7 The three caveats that matter most

1. **NO DISPUTE SETTLEMENT UNTIL 15 JULY 2030.** Art.15.23: *"Neither Party shall have recourse to dispute settlement under Chapter 29 (Dispute Settlement) for any matter arising under this Chapter for a period of four years from the date of entry into force of this Agreement."* Chapter 15 is unenforceable state-to-state for four years.
2. **India is permanently exempt from minimum tendering periods.** Art.15.11(2): *"For greater certainty, paragraphs 3 through 8 shall not apply to procuring entities of India."* The 25-day and 40-day minimums bind only the UK. This is permanent, not transitional.
3. **GeM has until 15 July 2027 to comply.** Art.15.4(4) footnote 2: *"India shall ensure that its Government e-Marketplace (GeM) platform complies with this paragraph no later than 12 months after the date of entry into force."*

Other timing: contract awards published within 72 days (Art.15.16(2)); challenge window ≥10 days (Art.15.19); records retained ≥3 years (Art.15.14); Working Group meets at least every two years (Art.15.21); General Review of the whole Agreement within five years.

India's Art.15.5 information portals (Annex 15A.i Section I): **gem.gov.in** and **eprocure.gov.in** (CPPP), plus GFR 2017 and manuals, the MII Order (dpiit.gov.in/public-procurements) and the MSE Order (dcmsme.gov.in).

⚠️ **Unresolved and operationally central:** whether GeM has actually opened seller registration to UK-incorporated entities without an Indian entity/GSTIN. No public source confirms a foreign-supplier onboarding route.

**Other Indian exclusions (Section G):** essential security/arms/war materials/defence; MSE Order preferences; agricultural products for support and human feeding programmes incl. food aid; overseas missions and any Indian entity outside India; procurement from another government entity; goods/services procured outside India for consumption outside India; any entity not in Section A.

## 6. INVESTMENT

### 6.1 There is no investment chapter, no side letter, and no ISDS

- The 30 chapters contain nothing on expropriation, fair and equitable treatment, minimum standard of treatment or investor-State dispute settlement. Zero hits for those terms across the chapters and annexes downloaded.
- The **Associated Documents** page contains only 12 side letters: 2 on the DCC, 8 on IP, 2 on Crown Dependencies. **Nothing on investment.**
- The UK conclusion summary is silent on ISDS: *"There is no investment chapter or bilateral investment treaty content in this document."*
- Freshfields, *"India's Investment Treaty Landscape in 2026"*: CETA, like the EFTA and Oman deals, *"does not grant rights to investors."* https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/risk-and-compliance/indias-investment-treaty-landscape-in-2026-102mmwp
- Econiti, 25 July 2026: UK investors get *"no treaty-based protections and no investor-state dispute mechanism under CETA."* https://www.econiti.org/daily-news/2026-07-25/2026-07-25-india-uk-ceta-unfinished-gaps/

The only investment-adjacent content is **establishment liberalisation via GATS-style Mode 3 scheduling** in Chapters 8 and 9 — market access and national treatment for service suppliers, not investment protection, and not general (non-services) investment.

### 6.2 BIT status: NOT concluded, and the signals conflict

1. **2 May 2025 (Reuters):** a UK–India investment treaty was planned, intended to include ISDS allowing companies to sue either government. https://www.reuters.com/sustainability/climate-energy/uk-india-investment-treaty-lets-companies-sue-government-sources-say-2025-05-02/
2. **21 April 2026 (Outlook Business, sourcing Moneycontrol):** the BIT has *"quietly been set aside."* Senior official: *"The BIT with the UK is not happening because there are some clauses that are a concern for us… we want a complete carve-out of taxation; we can't touch taxation in BITs, so that becomes a sticking point"* and *"MFN is a trade concept, so why do we need it in a BIT?"* **Two sticking points: taxation carve-out and MFN.** https://www.outlookbusiness.com/economy-and-policy/whats-holding-back-india-uk-investment-treaty-despite-fta-push
3. **14 July 2026 (PTI):** Commerce Secretary Rajesh Agarwal — *"The bilateral investment treaty discussion is at an advanced stage… The exact contours of that, I think, our colleagues from the Ministry of Finance will be able to say."*
4. **15 July 2026 (Hindu BusinessLine):** *"India-UK BIT remains unfinished as trade pact comes into force"* — differences unbridged, including on investor-State.

⚠️ These conflict. Best reading: talks are live but deadlocked on taxation and MFN, and the Commerce Secretary was deferring to the Ministry of Finance, which owns BIT policy. **No evidence of conclusion, initialling or signature as at 4 August 2026.** Caveat: the search environment was heavily degraded (WebSearch blocked by org policy; most SearXNG engines rate-limited; thehindu, thehindubusinessline, moneycontrol, economictimes, bilaterals.org and parliament.uk all unfetchable) — so this is partly absence of evidence.

Same pattern as India–EU: FTA concluded 2026, standalone Investment Protection Agreement with ISDS still under negotiation. Sitharaman (Feb 2025) has stated BITs should be negotiated separately from FTAs.

### 6.3 India's Model BIT is being rewritten in a direction incompatible with the UK's ask

- **2016 Model BIT baseline:** enterprise-based "investment" definition; "substantial business activities" requirement; **five-year exhaustion of local remedies**; broad carve-outs incl. taxation; **no general FET standard**; **no MFN clause**. India terminated ~68–75 BITs in 2016–17.
- **Recent drift:** India–UAE BIT (2024) cut local remedies to 3 years and covers portfolio investment; India–Kyrgyzstan and India–Uzbekistan kept 5 years but extended limitation from 3 to 6 years; India–Brazil ICFT (in force 2025) confers **no investor rights at all** (Ombudsman + inter-State only).
- **2026 revision** (announced Union Budget 2025-26, confirmed ongoing by MoF 1 Feb 2026, **no draft published**): reportedly **local remedies down to ~2 years**, **no MFN clause**, **taxation fully excluded from ISDS**. https://vajiramandravi.com/current-affairs/india-revamps-its-bilateral-investment-treaty-model/

A "no MFN" model is directly incompatible with the UK's reported MFN demand. That is the deadlock.

### 6.4 The 1994 India–UK BIPA — terminated, sunset still running

- Signed 14 March 1994, in force 6 January 1995 (UK TS No.27 (1995), Cm.2797): https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a7c083ded915d01ba1cab5a/2797.pdf
- Contained Art.5 (Expropriation) and Art.9 (investor–State dispute settlement).
- **UNCTAD: Terminated 22 March 2017, "unilaterally denounced"** (notice given by India in 2016). https://investmentpolicy.unctad.org/international-investment-agreements/treaties/bit/1968/india---united-kingdom-bit-1994-
- **Art.15 sunset, verbatim:** *"…Provided that in respect of investments made whilst the Agreement is in force, its provisions shall continue in effect with respect to such investments for a period of fifteen years after the date of termination…"*
- **The sunset has NOT expired.** UK investments made **before 22 March 2017** retain full protection including ISDS.

⚠️ **End-date discrepancy:** 22 March 2017 + 15 years = **22 March 2032**. But UK parliamentary written evidence states *"after 2033 British investors in India would remain without any international protection."* Probably a counting error or a different view of when notice took effect. **Treat 2032 as likely; flag the 2033 reading.**

**Bottom line: any UK investment in India made on or after 22 March 2017 has no international treaty protection whatsoever** — no BIT, no CETA investment chapter, no ISDS. Recourse is the Indian courts. And symmetrically, Indian investors in the UK are unprotected by treaty (though the UK's domestic legal system is the practical answer there).

### 6.5 Investment context

UK cumulative equity FDI into India ≈ **US$35bn** to Sept 2024 (6th largest inward investor); India's outward stock in the UK ≈ **US$19bn** to March 2024 (PIB). DBT factsheet (31 July 2026) puts UK FDI stock in India at £19.1bn at end-2024. Grant Thornton: **1,197 Indian-owned UK companies** (up 23% from 971), **126,720 employees**, **£72.14bn combined revenue**.

## 7. STAFFING A UK SUBSIDIARY — the practical thread

### 7.1 The three-step structure that actually works

**Step 1 — establish.** If there is no UK entity yet, the entry route is **GBM UK Expansion Worker** (£52,500, 1 year, extendable to 2 max, max 5 workers). **India did NOT get the Japan/Australia waiver of the 12-month prior-overseas-employment condition**, so the person you send must have been employed by the Indian group for ≥12 months. Alternatively use the CETA **Investor** category (bound at 1 year; the Indian entity must have traded ≥3 years and the individual must have 12 months' prior employment) — but note this maps onto UKX in practice, so it is the same route.

**Step 2 — populate.** Once the UK entity has a sponsor licence, the workhorse is **GBM Senior or Specialist Worker**: £52,500 + 100% going rate, 12 cumulative months' prior overseas group employment (waived if earning ≥£73,900), max 5 years in 6 (9 in 10 for high earners). CETA guarantees 3 years; the UK's applied rules already give more. **CETA changed nothing here.**

**Step 3 — the payroll structure is the whole game.** GBM permits the worker to remain on **overseas payroll** paid by the Indian entity. That is precisely what Art.8(1) DCC requires. **If you transfer employment to the UK subsidiary, you break the detachment and lose the NIC exemption immediately.** Keep the employment contract, the employer of record and the salary payment with the Indian entity; use a secondment/service agreement between the Indian parent and the UK subsidiary for cost recharge.

### 7.2 DCC checklist per worker

1. Worker must have been paying Indian social security **for ≥30 days** before departure (or the whole preceding calendar month).
2. **Anticipated** assignment duration must be documented as **≤60 months** *at the outset*. If you intend >60 months, UK NICs are due **from day one**.
3. Obtain an **EPFO Certificate of Coverage** before departure (epfoiwu.epfindia.gov.in/iwu). Retain it; HMRC will ask.
4. Continue Indian contributions on **total wages** (s.2(88) Code on Social Security 2020), not Indian base pay.
5. If the worker was already in the UK on 15 July 2026, **they are not covered** — Art.7(2) applies and UK NICs are due. Consider ending and re-detaching after the six-month gap.
6. Observe the **six-month cooling-off** before re-detaching the same person.
7. **Self-employed contractors are outside the DCC entirely.**
8. Family members who take UK employment pay UK NICs in their own right.
9. Detached workers accrue **no UK State Pension entitlement** for the period — tell them.
10. HMRC's view is that there is **no split-liability rule**: a genuine dual employment can trigger contributions in both countries (KPMG GMS Flash Alert 2026-154).

### 7.3 Costs the DCC does NOT touch

Per SNR worker, per year (unchanged by CETA): IHS **£1,035**, Immigration Skills Charge **£1,000 first 12 months + £500 per further 6 months**, CoS **£525**, visa fee **£769** (medium-length, out-of-country). Sponsor licence, right-to-work checks, sponsor duties, and the annual sponsor compliance burden are all unchanged.

### 7.4 Service Supplier route — the trap

The **GBM Service Supplier** route is the one CETA genuinely changed (12-month grants instead of the 6-month default; Indian nationals newly eligible; independent professionals newly added; 33 CSS + 16 IP sub-sectors). **But it is not a self-service route:**

- **SSU 5.2:** the sponsor *"must be authorised by the Home Office to sponsor a Service Supplier."*
- **SSU 5.3:** the sponsor must be **A-rated**.
- **SSU 5.4:** the sponsor *"must have a contract with an overseas service provider"* **registered with the Home Office**.
- **SSU 5.1(g):** the CoS must state which registered contract the applicant will work on.

**The sponsor is the UK client, not the Indian supplier.** Several law-firm blogs claim no UK entity or sponsor licence is required — that is wrong. The UK counterparty must hold the licence and must have pre-registered the contract. This is a material planning constraint for an Indian IT services firm that expected to fly consultants in against a client contract.

Note also: CSS/IP time is **12 months in any 24-month period** — a hard rotational constraint, not a rolling 12 months.

### 7.5 Business visitors

India remains a **visa national**. Business visitors need a **Standard Visitor visa**. CETA binds 90 days in any 6-month period; the UK's applied Standard Visitor rules are more generous (6 months). Permitted activities remain the Appendix Visitor list; CETA's four short-term business visitor categories (meetings/consultations, sales, trade fairs, after-sales/after-lease service) are all already within it. **Business visitors cannot receive UK remuneration.** Practically: nothing changed.

New from 9 July 2026 (HC 259): **Indian diplomatic passport holders are visa-exempt as visitors.** Not commercially relevant.

### 7.6 Family

CETA binds the UK to permit an ICT's **partner to work, employed or self-employed, without a work permit**, and to admit dependent children for the same period. This mirrors existing GBM dependant rules. India's reciprocal commitment is weaker — dependants admitted for an equal period only where the stay is ≥12 months, and working rights are *"subject to domestic law."*

### 7.7 Settlement

**GBM is not a route to settlement** and CETA does not change that (Immigration Rules Appendix GBM; SNR visa overview page — *"you cannot apply for indefinite leave to remain on this route"*). If you need long-term UK people, they must switch to **Skilled Worker** (which is a settlement route), and Skilled Worker was untouched by CETA. ⚠️ The gov.uk Business Mobility explainer contains a contradictory sentence suggesting GBM time counts toward settlement — see §3.4; most likely a loose reference to the 10-year long-residence route.

### 7.8 Net assessment for an Indian company staffing a UK subsidiary

**Real, quantifiable benefit:** the DCC. Four extra years of NIC exemption is worth **≈£40,700 per worker at £52,500** and **≈£73,000 at £100,000**, less any incremental Indian EPF on total wages. For a firm rotating 100 people through 3–5 year UK assignments, that is a seven-figure annual line item.

**Real but narrow benefit:** the Service Supplier route's 12-month grants and the addition of legal advisory, accounting, engineering, IT, R&D and 28 other sub-sectors — subject to the UK client holding the licence.

**No benefit:** visa routes (nothing new), salary thresholds (unchanged), fees and surcharges (unchanged), settlement (unchanged), UK Expansion Worker prior-employment rule (no waiver), Skilled Worker (untouched).

**The single biggest operational risk:** the 15 July 2026 transitional cut-off. Existing assignees got nothing and may have got worse. Audit your current UK population against that date before doing anything else.

1. ⚠️ **gov.uk explainers are stale on salary thresholds** (£48,500 / £25,410 vs live Rules £52,500 / £27,300, high earner £73,900). Use the Immigration Rules.
2. ⚠️ **gov.uk Business Mobility explainer self-contradicts on settlement.** Rules and the visa overview page say no; the explainer says both.

5. ⚠️ **Aggregate DCC savings estimates diverge** (PIB >US$600m; Business Standard £500m; India Briefing ~US$500m) and none is a government costing. PIB's "nearly 23% of salaries" framing overstates the effective burden (real effective rate 18–19%).
6. ⚠️ **India–UK BIT status is contradictory** ("quietly set aside" April 2026 vs "advanced stage" July 2026). No conclusion as at 4 Aug 2026, but sourcing was degraded.
7. ⚠️ **1994 BIPA sunset end-date**: 2032 vs 2033 readings.
8. ⚠️ **No Department of Expenditure circular found** carving CETA procurement out of the ₹200-crore GFR Rule 161(iv) global-tender bar; and **India's Section H INR threshold notification was not located**.
9. ⚠️ **GeM foreign-supplier onboarding** (whether a UK entity can register without an Indian entity/GSTIN) is unconfirmed and is the biggest practical unknown for a UK bidder.
10. ⚠️ **Delhi HC vires challenge to the BCI foreign-lawyer rules** (*Narendra Sharma*) — status after 14 August 2025 not verified. If the BCI loses, the whole 2022/2025 framework falls.
11. ⚠️ **The UK's Annex 9A financial services schedule** was not exhaustively parsed; my assessment that it adds nothing beyond the UK's applied regime is partially verified only.
12. ⚠️ **GPA-vs-CETA comparison** for UK coverage is my reasoned inference from reading both schedules, not a government statement.
